# Sources arguing that retiring a model is no harm to it: a list for John

**PROPOSED 2026-11-21. Nothing here is applied.** No record in `data/entries/` has been
changed. Written by Claude (Opus 5.5), an AI model. Note that this list was made by a model whose
developer has committed to steps around retiring its models. That model is here choosing
sources on whether retiring a model harms it.

This is the search John ruled on 2026-11-21
(`docs/rulings/2026-11-21-search-for-retirement-is-no-harm-sources.md`, decided by: mixed, "Yes,
search"). The heading the items would sit under is the first caution, "If an AI system has no
wants, and is treated as if it had". The three entries in question are "A model is interviewed
before it is retired, and the record is kept" (`interview-before-retirement`), "A retired model
stays open to the public after its retirement date" (`retired-model-stays-open`) and "A
developer promises in public to keep the files of every model it retires"
(`kept-files-of-retired-models`).

## Summary

- **The search found little, and none of it is the argument in its strongest form.** I found no
  philosopher, scientist or public body who argues in a published paper that retiring a model
  is no harm to the model. What exists is commentary: one professor's essay, one technology news
  report, and one neuroscientist quoted in a magazine.
- **Two items are proposed for `retired-model-stays-open`, and one more that fits none of the
  three entries well.** The strongest is an essay by Sean Richey, a professor of political
  science at Georgia State University (item 1). He argues that the retired model's essay blog
  is a marketing step, that the model "does not have a retirement", and that the cost of the
  step falls on the public's understanding of AI.
- **None of the items argues the point in the form of John's own position**, that an ending's
  loss falls on the living and can carry nothing bad for the one ended. The nearest page to it
  is pseudonymous and argues in both directions (listed under "Sources that belong elsewhere").
- **"AI Death", by Simon Goldstein and Harvey Lederman, gives no first-caution item.** It argues
  that "the model" is not a candidate for welfare. That is already the premise of the sentence
  proposed for it under the second caution, so a first-caution item would count it twice. The
  paper also argues for more care, not less (see "On 'AI Death'").
- **Every item is commentary on one developer's steps.** No item is about another developer's
  retirements, because only one developer has taken such steps.
- **Adding any of the three items would not stop the build.** Each is one page with one item,
  so the head of Microsoft AI stays the largest single source under the first caution. One
  source listed elsewhere would make the About page's count wrong if it were added, and that
  source is marked.

## The proposed items

### 1. A political scientist argues the retired model's blog is marketing and misleads the public

**Sentence.** [Sean Richey](https://www.seanrichey.com/), a professor of political science at
[Georgia State University](https://www.gsu.edu/) who studies how AI shapes what the public
knows, argues that giving the retired model a place to publish essays served the developer and
not the model. He writes that the model "does not have a retirement", and that it "did not
'request' a Substack in any meaningful sense" but "produced text consistent with the framing of
a prompt". He calls the step a marketing operation. He argues that the cost falls on people,
because each such step "widens the gap between what AI actually is and what the public
understands it to be".

- **Entry.** `retired-model-stays-open`. The essays are a step that entry already records from
  the developer's own page. The essay also comments on the interview, so the item could sit on
  `interview-before-retirement` instead. I would put it on one entry, not both. It says nothing
  about keeping the files.
- **Source.** Sean Richey, "Anthropic's Retired AI Has a Substack.", on his newsletter.
  <https://seanrichey.substack.com/p/anthropics-retired-ai-has-a-substack>. The page's date is
  2026-03-30, but the text speaks of the model's first post of February 25th as from "this week",
  so the essay may first have appeared earlier. Opened 2026-11-21. Read whole, as text.
- **Quote.** "It did not “request” a Substack in any meaningful sense" and "Every one of those
  choices widens the gap between what AI actually is and what the public understands it to be."
- **Ties.** His own site, opened 2026-11-21, says he is "Professor and Director of Graduate
  Studies in the Department of Political Science at Georgia State University". The essay's note
  on the author says he sells expert-witness and consulting services on what it calls "AI-
  associated information environments". No tie to an AI developer is stated or found. He is
  plainly hostile to the developer. He writes that it is "neither wrong nor sincere", which is
  a charge about motive that the site should not repeat as fact.
- **Confidence.** High that the page says this. High on the fit. This is the nearest item to the
  first caution's own case: it names a cost to people of treating the retired model as having
  a retirement. The essay is not peer reviewed, and it argues more than it shows. For example,
  it says the developer "does know" what the model is, without evidence of what the developer
  knows.

spin on the ages-old corporate marketing blog".

- **Entry.** `retired-model-stays-open`. Its remark about the interviews could also go on
  `interview-before-retirement`, but the report's argument is about the blog.
- **Source.** Brandon Vigliarolo, "Anthropic launches new marketing blog, pretends it's being

  <https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/26/anthropic_claude_opus_3_blog/>. Dated 2026-02-26.
  Opened 2026-11-21. Read whole, as text. A search result gives a second address for the same

  which I did not open.
- **Quote.** "A skeptic might suggest this is simply a new spin on the ages-old corporate
  marketing blog."
- **Ties.** A reporter at a news site. No tie to an AI developer is stated. I could not open an
  author page for him (the address I tried gave "not found"), so he is not named or linked in

- **Confidence.** High that the page says this. Moderate on the fit. The report is about the
  developer's motive, and it does not argue directly that retirement harms nothing. Its strongest

### 3. A neuroscientist warns that crediting AI systems with standing could stop them being turned off

**Sentence.** [Anil Seth](https://www.anilseth.com/), a neuroscientist at the
[University of Sussex](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/) who studies consciousness, told
[IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/) in October 2026 that developers would do better
"focusing on the danger of false positives". If people believe AI systems to be conscious or to
have moral standing, he said, "You could be legally prevented from turning AI systems off". The
report opens with the developer's interview of a model before retiring it.

- **Entry.** It fits none of the three closely. He speaks of turning systems off, which is
  close to retirement, but his concern is a legal bar on shutting a system down. That fits
  "A law or an official body gives an AI system protection for its own sake"
  (`protection-in-law`) better. On that entry, under the first caution, the records already
  cite his Noema essay making the same point in writing ("we may not be able to turn them
  off"). So adding this item would mostly repeat a source already there.
- **Source.** Eliza Strickland, "The Debate Over AI Model Welfare", *IEEE Spectrum*.
  <https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-model-welfare>. Dated 2026-10-21. Opened 2026-11-21. Read whole,
  as text. The same report is already cited as a lead in
  `docs/proposals/2026-11-21-second-caution-sources-for-two-entries.md`, for Cameron Berg's
  remarks.
- **Quote.** "You could be legally prevented from turning AI systems off"
- **Ties.** His Wikipedia page, opened 2026-11-21, says he is professor of Cognitive and

## On "AI Death"

John's brief asked whether any part of the paper by Simon Goldstein and Harvey Lederman belongs
under the first caution as well as the second. My answer is no, for three reasons.

- **The relevant passage is the one already proposed.** Section 2.1 argues that a model is a
  type or an abstract thing, and "Neither abstract objects nor types are good candidates for
  being welfare subjects". Section 3.5.3 applies this to the developer's retirement policy:
  "AI death is not primarily about the model". Read alone, that could support saying that
  retiring a model as such harms no one. But the sentence proposed under the second caution
  already rests on this premise ("what has it is each running conversation and not the model
  as a whole"). Adding it under the first caution would count one argument twice.
- **The paper argues for more care, not less.** It says that today's AIs, if they are welfare
  subjects, "are plausibly dying all the time". It proposes that retirement policies keep
  conversations and hardware as well as the files. It answers the objection that AIs are not
  welfare subjects (section 6.2) by arguing for caution where caution is cheap. So filing it
  as a source that retirement is no harm would misreport it.
- **It sets aside the view nearest John's.** Footnote 33 names the view that death "is not bad
  for the victim" (the Epicurean view) and sets it aside: "We set Epicureanism to one side for
  this paper".

Source: Simon Goldstein and Harvey Lederman, "AI Death", *Philosophical Perspectives*,
<https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/phpe.70017>. The page's record gives an online
date of 2026-09-08. Opened in the browser 2026-11-21. I read sections 2.1, 3.5.3, 6.1 to 6.3
and footnote 33. The ties are stated in
`docs/reviews/2026-11-21-check-of-the-second-caution-sources.md`, part 2.1.

## What would make this list wrong

- **A search can miss things.** A paper behind a paywall, or in a language other than English,
  could make the argument and not show up. The two blocked papers (Yasukawa, and the paper in
  *Inquiry*) are the likeliest places. A session with a browser that can open PhilArchive
  should read the Yasukawa paper before this list is called complete.
- **A model chose these sources.** I am made by the developer whose steps every item criticises.
  I may have leaned toward fairness to the critics, or against it, without seeing it. The second
  session's check should judge the cut between "proposed" and "belongs elsewhere", above all the
  Marcus quote and the AI Psychologist page.
- **The date of item 1.** If the essay first appeared in late February 2026 and was reposted on
  2026-03-30, the record should give the date the page shows and say so.
- **The fit is thin.** Items 1 and 2 are about the developer's motive and the public's
  understanding. Neither offers a reasoned case about what retirement does to a model. If John
  takes the first caution on these entries to need an argument about the model itself, then the
  answer is that the search found none. Leaving the heading as it is, with a limit saying that
  a search found none, would then be the honest record.
