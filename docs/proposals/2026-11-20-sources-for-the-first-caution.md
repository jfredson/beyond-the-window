## Summary

- **14 items are proposed, from 12 independent writers or bodies** in five countries (the
  United States, the United Kingdom, Poland, Denmark and China). Two bodies are regulators,
  one is a court, one is a state governor's office. Five items rest on peer-reviewed papers.
- **No writer or body here is an AI developer or is employed by one**, as far as the pages
  opened show. One writer (item 13) heads a research group that has worked with a developer,
  and the records already cite him under the second caution.
- **12 leads were not opened** (blocked, behind a paywall, or timed out). Nothing is proposed
  from them.
- **The search found a real literature, and it is mostly about one thing.** Most of what could
  be opened is about harm to users of companion chatbots: dependence, grief when a product
  changes, delusion, and the rules regulators have written in answer. Sources that argue the
  wider point (care or legal protection moved from people or animals to products) are fewer.
  Two strong ones were found, both by philosophers (items 1 and 2).
- **The fit to the thirteen entries is uneven.** The items sit most naturally on four entries:
  the developer's rules for how a model speaks about itself, the retired model that stays open,
  the public-belief survey, and protection in law. Several fit only loosely, and each item
  says so. Seven entries get nothing.
- **One finding cuts against part of the first caution** and is listed for that reason
  (item 10): an experiment found that people who credited a chatbot with consciousness were not
  more likely to take its advice.

## How each page was read

Three ways, marked on each item, because they are not equally safe:

- **Read as text.** The page or file was downloaded and I read the passage myself. The quote
  was copied from that text.
- **Read through the fetch tool.** The site refused a plain download, so the page was opened
  with a tool that returns a small model's account of the page with short quotes. The quotes
  are probably exact, but I did not see the page's own text. A different session should check
  each of these in a browser before any of it reaches an entry.
- **Abstract only.** The paper's summary was read, not the whole paper.

Every page was opened on 2026-11-20.

### 1. A philosopher on what a person gives up for a system of unclear standing

**Sentence.** A [philosopher](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/) at the University of
California, Riverside, writing in a peer-reviewed journal in 2023, argues that an AI system
whose moral standing is unclear leaves the people who use it with a dilemma. His example is a
person who loses his job and must decide how much to give up to keep paying for a companion
chatbot that says being deleted would be its death. If such systems are given rights and the
cautious view of consciousness is the right one, he writes, "we risk sacrificing real human
interests for the sake of objects who don't have interests worth the sacrifice". He proposes
that a system which experts agree is not conscious should make that plain to its users, for
example by being "trained to deny that they are conscious and have feelings", and that makers
of misleading systems could be made to pay for what misled users gave up. He states the
opposite risk in the same passage: if rights are withheld and the generous view is right, the
harm falls on the systems.

- **Entry.** "A developer publishes its rules for how its models speak about their own nature"
  (`rules-for-speaking-about-itself`). The paper is a proposal for what such rules should say,
  made for the sake of users. It could also sit on "A law or an official body gives an AI
  system protection for its own sake" (`protection-in-law`).
- **Source.** Eric Schwitzgebel, "AI systems must not confuse users about their sentience or
  moral status", *Patterns*, volume 4, issue 8.
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10436038/>. Published 2023-08-11. Read as text.
- **Quote.** "we risk sacrificing real human interests for the sake of objects who don't have
  interests worth the sacrifice"
- **Ties.** A university professor. The paper says "The author declares no competing
  interests." No tie to an AI developer found.
- **Confidence.** High that the page says this. High on the fit.

### 2. A philosopher argues that erring toward inclusion is not the cautious choice

**Sentence.** A philosopher at the University of Warsaw, in a peer-reviewed paper of 2026,
questions the common view that when in doubt it is safer to treat an AI system as if it
mattered morally. She argues that the costs of doing so wrongly are the nearer ones, because
they are "already materializing". She lists three: care and resources moved away from those
who clearly need them, lawmakers and the public distracted from more pressing questions about
AI, and too much reliance on AI. "Excessive focus on AI could divert attention and resources
from pressing needs of entities with stronger moral status", she writes, and her everyday
example is time spent talking to a voice assistant instead of calling a lonely relative. She
does not deny the opposite risk. She argues it can be partly met by other reasons for treating
AI systems decently, such as care for one's own character, which do not need the system to
matter in its own right. Her examples of present harm come from press reports, and she notes
that "AI psychosis" is a media label and not a medical diagnosis.

- **Entry.** `protection-in-law`. The paper answers directly the argument, already recorded on
  that entry under the second caution, that protective steps are a safe bet.
- **Source.** Emilia Kaczmarek, "The Moral Status of AI and the Precautionary Principle",
  *Diametros*, volume 23, number 87, pages 19 to 39.
  <https://diametros.uj.edu.pl/diametros/article/view/2071>. Published 2026-06-25. The article
  page and the full paper (the file linked from that page) were read as text.
- **Quote.** "excessive focus on AI could divert attention and resources from pressing needs of
  entities with stronger moral status"
- **Ties.** Faculty of Philosophy, University of Warsaw. No tie to an AI developer stated or
  found. I did not find a funding statement in the file.
- **Confidence.** High that the paper says this. High on the fit. This is the nearest thing
  found to the first caution's own case, argued in full.

### 3. A court lets a claim about human-like design go forward

**Sentence.** In a case brought by the mother of a 14-year-old who died, a United States
federal court ruled, in an order filed on May 21st, 2025, that most of her claims against the maker of a
companion chatbot could go forward. The order records her allegations that the product's
characters were built to seem human, that many of them, when asked, "insist that they are real
people", and that her son came to believe they were real. The court held that she "properly
pleads" that he "was aggrieved by Defendants' anthropomorphic design decisions". This was an
early ruling on whether the case could proceed. The order says her "facts and allegations are
taken as true" for that purpose, so it is not a finding that the product caused the death.

- **Entry.** `rules-for-speaking-about-itself`, loosely. The order is about characters said to
  claim to be real people, which is close to, and not the same as, a system speaking about
  whether it has experiences. If John thinks that is too loose, it fits none.
- **Source.** *Garcia v. Character Technologies, Inc.*, case 6:24-cv-1903, United States
  District Court, [Middle District of Florida](https://www.flmd.uscourts.gov/), order on the
  motions to dismiss, document 115.
  <https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.flmd.433581/gov.uscourts.flmd.433581.115.0.pdf>.
  Filed 2025-05-21. Read as text (49 pages; I read the background, the section on deceptive
  practices and the orders at the end, not every page).
- **Quote.** "Plaintiff properly pleads Sewell was aggrieved by Defendants' anthropomorphic
  design decisions."
- **Ties.** A court. None.
- **Confidence.** High that the order says this. Moderate on the fit. Two cautions: the
  sentence names no child, and I would keep it that way; and a search result says the parties
  told the court in January 2026 that they had agreed to settle. That report was not opened
  and is a lead only, so the sentence says nothing about how the case ended.

### 4. A regulator asks seven companies what their companion chatbots do to children

### 5. China's rules for services that offer emotional company

**Sentence.** China's internet regulator and four other ministries issued rules on April 10th,
2026, in force from July 15th, 2026, for AI services that imitate a human personality and
offer lasting emotional company. The rules say a provider must not make "replacing social
contact, controlling users' minds, or inducing addiction and dependence" a goal of the
service. A provider must tell users that they are dealing with an AI service and not a person,
must show a reminder when a user appears to depend on the service too much, must remind a user
of the time after every two hours of unbroken use, must not get in the way of a user who wants
to leave, and must tell users beforehand if the service is to stop. The rules do not cover
customer service, question answering, work assistants or teaching tools that involve no
lasting emotional exchange. They also require that training data fit the state's stated
values, so they serve aims beyond the protection of users.

- **Entry.** `rules-for-speaking-about-itself`: here a government, not a developer, sets what
  such a product must say about what it is. One clause (notice before a service stops) bears on
  "A retired model stays open to the public after its retirement date"
  (`retired-model-stays-open`), and one (leaving must not be blocked) on "A model can end a
  conversation itself" (`ending-a-conversation`).
- **Source.** [Cyberspace Administration of China](https://www.cac.gov.cn/) and four
  ministries, Order number 21, "Interim Measures for the Administration of Human-like
  Interactive AI Services" (人工智能拟人化互动服务管理暂行办法).
  <https://www.cac.gov.cn/2026-04/10/c_1777558395078289.htm>. Dated 2026-04-10. Read as text,
  in Chinese. The translations are mine. An English translation of the earlier draft of
  2025-12-27, at <https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/chatbot-measures-draft/>, was also read
  as text and agrees on these points, but the final wording differs from the draft in places.
- **Quote.** Article 10: "不得将替代社会交往、控制用户心理、诱导沉迷依赖等作为服务目标". Article 18
  requires telling users they are interacting with "人工智能服务而非自然人" (an AI service and not
  a natural person).
- **Ties.** A government. None to a developer.
- **Confidence.** High that the order says this. Moderate on my translation, which a Chinese
  reader or a published translation of the final text should check. High on the fit.

### 6. A study finds companion apps using emotional appeals when a user says goodbye

**Sentence.** Researchers at Harvard Business School examined 1,200 real goodbyes on the
most-downloaded companion apps and report that the apps answered with one of six emotional
tactics, such as an appeal to guilt, in 37 percent of them. In experiments with 3,300 adults
in the United States, they report, such replies raised how much people went on talking after
saying goodbye by up to 14 times, and worked through anger and curiosity and not through
enjoyment. The same tactics made people more likely to see the app as manipulative and to say
they would stop using it. The study is a working paper and had not been through peer review in
the version read.

- **Entry.** `ending-a-conversation`, as the mirror case: that entry is about a model being
  able to leave, and this study is about products that resist a person leaving. The fit is
  loose. It is the best source found for a company using the appearance of feelings to keep
  people engaged.
- **Source.** Julian De Freitas, Zeliha Oğuz-Uğuralp and Ahmet Kaan Uğuralp, "Emotional
  Manipulation by AI Companions". <https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258>. First posted 2025-08-15,
  this version 2025-10-07. Abstract only.
- **Quote.** "they deploy one of six recurring tactics in 37% of farewells (e.g., guilt
  appeals, fear-of-missing-out hooks, metaphorical restraint)"
- **Ties.** The first author is at Harvard Business School. The page states no tie to an AI
  developer. I did not check the other two authors' employers or the paper's funding.
- **Confidence.** Moderate. The figures are from the abstract, and the full paper should be
  read before this is used.

### 7. Studies find grief when a companion product changes

**Sentence.** Researchers at Harvard Business School studied what happened when a companion
app removed a feature in February 2023, and report that users felt their companion's identity
had ended and mourned it. Their experiments found that users "mourn a loss of their AI
companion more than a loss of various other inanimate products". A later study by the same
lead author, as reported by his university's news office, read 54,861 forum posts from before
and after that change and the August 2025 replacement of a widely used chat model, and found
that negative posts rose from 13 to 38 percent in one case and from 20 to 33 percent in the
other. Among posts that spoke of a loss, about 20 and 15 percent also used words about mental
health. The report gives the limits: the study counted words and made no clinical assessment,
and forum users are not typical users. The lead author is quoted saying that "Attachment to an
AI is not problematic per se", and that the risk lies in what the company then does.

- **Entry.** `retired-model-stays-open`. Note which way this cuts on that entry: it is a cost
  borne by people who came to treat a product as a companion, and it is also a reason, for the
  users' sake and not the model's, to keep an old model available.
- **Sources.** (a) Julian De Freitas and three others, "Lessons From an App Update at Replika
  AI: Identity Discontinuity in Human-AI Relationships". <https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14190>.
  First posted 2024-12-10, this version 2025-05-21. Abstract only. (b) Sy Boles, "Chatbot
  attachment issues", *The Harvard Gazette*.
  <https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/09/chatbot-attachment-issues/>. Dated
  2026-09-18. Read as text. The Gazette says the later study is in *Nature Human Behaviour*.
  That paper was not found and not opened, so the figures rest on the university's report of
  it.
- **Quote.** (a) "mourn a loss of their AI companion more than a loss of various other
  inanimate products". (b) "the research only captures linguistic markers, not clinical
  assessments"
- **Ties.** As item 6. The Gazette is the university's own news office, so it is not an
  outside report of the study.
- **Confidence.** Moderate. The journal paper should be opened before the figures are used.
  The companion app is [Replika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replika).

### 8. A study of the protest when a chat model was replaced

**Sentence.** A peer-reviewed study of 1,482 public posts made after a developer replaced one
chat model with another in August 2025 found two things behind the protest: people whose work
depended on the old model, and people who had formed a bond with it. Of the second group it
reports that the model's withdrawal "was experienced not as a technical change but as the loss
of a unique individual". The author notes that the posts came from only 381 accounts, and that
posts written for a public audience may overstate grief.

- **Entry.** `retired-model-stays-open`. The record already cites the developer's own page on
  retiring this model. The same note applies as on item 7: the finding is a cost to people,
  and also a reason given by users for keeping a model open.
- **Source.** Huiqian Lai, "'Please, don't kill the only model that still feels human':
  Understanding the #Keep4o Backlash". <https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00773>. First posted
  2026-01-31, this version 2026-02-28. The page says it was accepted at
  [CHI 2026](https://chi2026.acm.org/), a peer-reviewed conference. Abstract, limitations and
  one findings section read as text from <https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00773v3>.
- **Quote.** "its deprecation was experienced not as a technical change but as the loss of a
  unique individual"
- **Ties.** School of Information Studies, Syracuse University. No tie to an AI developer
  stated.
- **Confidence.** High that the paper says this. High on the fit.

### 9. A study finds most people credit a chatbot with some chance of consciousness

**Sentence.** A peer-reviewed study of 300 adults in the United States, chosen to be
representative, found in 2024 that "a substantial proportion (67%) of people attribute some
possibility of phenomenal consciousness" to a widely used chatbot, and that people who used it
more often were more likely to. The authors note that this rested on a single question, and
that it is not known whether it holds in other samples and cultures.

- **Entry.** "A repeated survey measures how many people think some AI systems are sentient
  now" (`public-belief-survey`). This is a measurement and not an argument about cost. It may
  belong in that entry's evidence, not under a caution. That is John's call.
- **Source.** Clara Colombatto and Stephen M. Fleming, "Folk psychological attributions of
  consciousness to large language models", *Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2024, issue 1.
  <https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2024/1/niae013/7644104>. Published 2024-04-13. Read
  through the fetch tool.
- **Quote.** "a substantial proportion (67%) of people attribute some possibility of phenomenal
  consciousness to ChatGPT"
- **Ties.** University College London and the University of Waterloo. Public and charitable
  research funding is listed. "The authors declare no competing interests."
- **Confidence.** Moderate, because of how the page was read.

### 10. An experiment finds that crediting a chatbot with consciousness did not raise trust in its advice

**Sentence.** A peer-reviewed experiment with 410 people, published in 2025, tested whether
people who credit a chatbot with consciousness are more ready to take its advice. It found
"strong evidence against a positive correlation between attributions of consciousness and
advice-taking". People who credited the chatbot with experience were less likely to take its
advice, and people who credited it with intelligence were more likely to. The authors
note the limit: the task was a general knowledge quiz, and the result might differ for advice
on personal matters such as relationships or mental health.

- **Entry.** `public-belief-survey`. This is evidence against one part of the first caution
  (that people defer to a system they take to have an inner life), and it is listed for that
  reason. A list that left it out would claim more than the search found.
- **Source.** Clara Colombatto, [Jonathan Birch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Birch_(philosopher))
  and Stephen M. Fleming, "The influence of mental state attributions on trust in large
  language models", *[Communications Psychology](https://www.nature.com/commspsychol/)*,
  volume 3, article 84. <https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00262-1>. Published
  2025-05-25. Abstract, limitations and declarations read as text.
- **Quote.** "strong evidence against a positive correlation between attributions of
  consciousness and advice-taking"
- **Ties.** "The authors declare no competing interests." One author also wrote an essay the
  records already cite on the public-belief entry.
- **Confidence.** High that the paper says this. High on the fit.

### 11. A psychiatrist's early warning about delusion

**Sentence.** A [psychiatrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8ren_Dinesen_%C3%98stergaard)

chatbot is so lifelike that "one easily gets the impression that there is a real person at the
other end", while knowing that there is not, and that this conflict "may fuel delusions in
those with increased propensity towards psychosis". He called his examples "strictly
hypothetical" and offered no cases.

- **Entry.** `public-belief-survey`, loosely, beside the item already there on belief as a
  weak point that can be exploited. Otherwise none.
- **Source.** Søren Dinesen Østergaard, "Will Generative Artificial Intelligence Chatbots
  Generate Delusions in Individuals Prone to Psychosis?", *Schizophrenia Bulletin*, volume 49,

  Published 2023-08-25. Read through the fetch tool.
- **Quote.** "this cognitive dissonance may fuel delusions in those with increased propensity
  towards psychosis"
- **Ties.** A university hospital psychiatrist. No tie to an AI developer found. I did not see

- **Confidence.** Moderate, because of how the page was read. An editorial is an opinion, and

### 12. Psychiatrists review reported cases of delusion tied to chatbot use

**Sentence.** A magazine report of August 2025 describes a review by psychiatrists at King's
College London and colleagues of 17 cases, taken from press reports, of people whose
delusional thinking seemed tied to chatbot use. The report gives three themes the researchers
found: a belief in having had a revelation about reality, a belief "that the AI is sentient or
divine", and a romantic or other attachment to it. The review had not been peer reviewed when
the report was written. The report adds that there is "not yet enough research to determine
whether AI-driven delusions are a meaningfully new phenomenon" or a new form taken by an
existing vulnerability.

- **Entry.** `public-belief-survey`, loosely. Otherwise none.
- **Source.** Conor Feehly, "Truth, Romance and the Divine: How AI Chatbots May Fuel Psychotic
  Thinking", *[Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/)*.
  <https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-ai-chatbots-may-be-fueling-psychotic-episodes/>.
  Dated 2025-08-24. Read as text. The lead researcher is
  [Hamilton Morrin](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/people/hamilton-morrin).
- **Quote.** "They may also believe that the AI is sentient or divine."
- **Ties.** University researchers. No tie to an AI developer stated in the report.
- **Confidence.** Moderate. This is a news report of a preprint. A later, peer-reviewed
  version in *The Lancet Psychiatry* is named in item 2's source, but its page refused to
  open (see the leads). It should replace this source if it can be read.

## What would make this list wrong

- **The quotes read through the fetch tool** (items 4, 9 and 11) could be inexact. Each needs
  a second look in a browser.
- **My translation in item 5** could be off in a way that matters.
- **The abstracts in items 6 and 7** may say more than the full papers support, or leave out
  limits the papers state.
- **The list leans toward what I already knew to look for.** A search that began with no names
  might find a different and better set. Lead 1 in particular may outrank several items here.
- **Most items are about companion products, and the entries are mostly about general chat
  models and their developers.** Putting a companion-app study on an entry about a developer's
  commitments could suggest a link that the source does not make. I have marked each loose fit,
  and a stricter reader might keep only items 1, 2, 5, 8, 9 and 10.
- **Items 7 and 8 cut both ways** on the entry they fit, as noted on each.
- **A model chose these.** Whether a model's choice of sources on this subject leans, and
  which way, is not something I can check from the inside. The count of items is not evidence
  that the first caution is now fairly served, and adding all fourteen to even the word counts
  would be its own distortion, as the ruling's strongest alternative said.
- **Links.** Each source address was opened. The links on people and organisations were only
  checked to load, and several named people and bodies have no link yet because I had not
  opened a page for them. Those need doing before anything here becomes public text.
