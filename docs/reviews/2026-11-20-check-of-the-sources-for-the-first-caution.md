## The fourteen items

For each: whether the sentence holds, whether it is about the first caution, which entry it
fits, and the ties.

### 1. A philosopher on what a person gives up for a system of unclear standing

- **Verdict: holds with a rewording, of one word.** Every claim is in the paper: the dilemma,
  the person who can no longer pay for a companion chatbot that says deletion would be death,
  the quoted risk, the proposal that systems which are not conscious "should be trained to
  deny that they are conscious and have feelings", the liability of makers, and the opposite
  risk in the same passage.
- **The rewording:** the paper's person, Sam, is given no gender, and is "suddenly fired".
  For "a person who loses his job" write "a person who loses their job".
- **About the first caution?** Yes, squarely. It is the cost to a person of treating a system
  as owed something when it may be owed nothing.
- **Entry:** the entry on a developer's rules for how its models speak about their own nature
  is right. The paper is a proposal for what such rules should say.
- **Ties:** confirmed. A professor of philosophy at the University of California, Riverside,
  and "The author declares no competing interests."
- **One thing the proposal does not say:** the About page already cites this writer, for a
  different paper, in its general statement of the two opposite mistakes.

### 2. A philosopher argues that erring toward inclusion is not the cautious choice

- **Verdict: holds as worded.** The paper lists three risks of crediting an AI system with
  too much standing, in the order the sentence gives, and the quotation is exact. It says the
  risks are "already materializing", that the opposite risk exists and can be partly met by
  other reasons such as "concern for one's own moral character", and that "AI psychosis" is
  "a media label" and not an official diagnosis. Two words differ from the paper and change
  nothing: it says "voicebot" and "a lonely family member".
- **About the first caution?** Yes. This is the first caution's case argued in full.
- **Entry:** the entry on protection in law is right.
- **Ties:** confirmed. Faculty of Philosophy, University of Warsaw. I also found no statement
  of funding in the file.
- **One thing the proposal does not say:** the About page already cites this same paper. On
  an entry it would be said a second time.

### 3. A court lets a claim about human-like design go forward

- **Verdict: holds with a rewording.** The order says the facts "are taken as true for the
  purposes of this Order", records the allegation that many characters "insist that they are
  real people", and says the plaintiff "alleges (both specifically and by implication)" that
  her son believed the characters were real. The quoted holding is exact.
- **The rewording:** the sentence says "most of her claims" could go forward. The order
  grants the motions in part and denies them in part, and I confirmed that one claim failed
  and two were allowed. I did not count them all. Write "ruled that the case could go forward
  on several of her claims".
- **About the first caution?** Near it. The order is about a product said to pass itself off
  as a person, and a child said to have believed it. That is treating a system as someone. It
  is not about wants or feelings, and the claim that succeeded is one of deceptive trade
  practice.
- **Entry:** it fits none of the thirteen. The proposal says so itself.
- **Ties:** a court. None.

### 4. A regulator asks seven companies what their companion chatbots do to children

- **Verdict: holds as worded.** I read the whole announcement. The sentence the searcher saw
  in pieces reads in full that such chatbots "generally are designed to communicate like a
  friend or confidant, which may prompt some users, especially children and teens, to trust
  and form relationships with chatbots". The announcement says the orders use a power for
  studies "that do not have a specific law enforcement purpose".
- **About the first caution?** Near it. The concern is harm to children from products built
  to seem like a friend. It does not turn on whether anyone takes the product to have
  feelings.
- **Entry:** fits none.
- **Ties:** confirmed. Two of the seven companies are developers the records name. A third,
  the maker of the companion chatbot in item 3, is among them too.

### 5. China's rules for services that offer emotional company

- **Verdict: holds with a rewording.** I read the Chinese text, and every point in the
  sentence is in it: the five issuing bodies, the two dates, the scope, the ban on certain
  goals, the notice that the user is dealing with an AI service and not a person, the
  reminder to a user who seems to depend on it, the reminder every two hours, the ban on
  hindering a user who wants to leave, and notice before a service stops.
- **A published English translation of the final text exists.** It is on China Law Translate,
  a site of translated Chinese law, under the title "Provisional Measures on the
  Administration of Human-like Interactive Artificial Intelligence Services"
  (<https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/human-like-ai/>). I read it whole. It agrees with the
  searcher's translation on every point. A law firm's summary of the final rules agrees too.
  It is not an official translation, and I found none.
- **The rewording:** quote the published translation and not the searcher's own words. Its
  wording of the ban is that "the goals of the service must not include replacing social
  interactions, psychologically controlling users, or inducing addiction". Two smaller
  points: the list of services left out also names scientific research, and where notice of
  a service stopping cannot be given beforehand, the provider must publish a notice promptly.
- **How far I can confirm the wording:** I am the same kind of model as the searcher, so my
  reading of the Chinese is not an independent one. The published translation is.
- **About the first caution?** In part. The rules are about dependence and about users
  knowing they are dealing with a machine. They say nothing of a system's wants.
- **Entry:** it fits the entry on a developer's rules only loosely, because these are a
  government's rules for what a product must say it is, and not a developer's rules for how a
  model speaks of its own nature. I would not force it.
- **Ties:** a government. None to a developer.

### 6. A study finds companion apps using emotional appeals when a user says goodbye

- **Verdict: holds with a rewording.** The figures are in the paper: 1,200 goodbyes, 37
  percent, 3,300 adults, "up to 14x", anger and curiosity and not enjoyment, and the cost to
  the app in users who see it as manipulative and say they would leave. It is a working
  paper.
- **The rewording, two points.** First, the goodbyes were not users' own. The researchers
  themselves chatted with six apps and "sent a farewell statement randomly selected from a
  predefined set", 200 to each app. Write "sent 1,200 goodbyes to six of the most-downloaded
  companion apps". Second, only the first author is at Harvard Business School. The paper
  lists the other two at Marsdata Academic, and one of them also at a company. Write "A
  researcher at Harvard Business School and two co-authors".
- **About the first caution?** No. It is about a product's design holding a user who wants to
  leave. The paper mentions, in passing, apps "perceived as sentient", and measures nothing
  about that.
- **Entry:** fits none. The entry on ending a conversation is about a model being able to
  end one, and the mirror is the searcher's own.
- **Ties:** corrected as above. The paper says "Funding for this research was provided in part
  by Harvard Business School."

### 9. A study finds most people credit a chatbot with some chance of consciousness

- **Verdict: holds with a rewording.** The quotation is exact, the link with how often people

- **The rewording, two points.** The survey "was conducted in July 2023" and published in

### 10. An experiment finds that crediting a chatbot with consciousness did not raise trust in its advice

- **Verdict: holds with a rewording.** This is the item that cuts against the first caution,
  and it should stay. The quoted finding is exact, and the stated limit about personal
  matters "such as relationships or mental health" is there.
- **The rewording, three points.** "Less likely to take its advice" should be "slightly less
  likely": the paper calls it "a small negative correlation". The 410 were adults in the
  United States. And the advice did not come from the chatbot: participants "were told that
  the advice was generated by ChatGPT, although the advice was in fact predetermined". A
  reader should be told that.
- **About the first caution?** Yes, as evidence against one part of it: that people defer to
  a system they take to have an inner life.
- **Entry:** the entry on the repeated public survey, as evidence.
- **Ties:** to be added to. One author is the philosopher whose essay the records cite on
  that entry, and the records say that writer is one of the ten authors of a 2024 report led
  by the head of Eleos AI Research. So this paper is not clear of the group that most
  second-caution items come from.

### 14. A state law requires companion chatbots to say what they are

- **Verdict: holds with a rewording.** The sentence quotes the governor's office correctly.
  The law itself, which I read, is narrower than the announcement.
- **What the law says.** A notice that the chatbot is "artificially generated and not human"

  knows is a minor, it must disclose that the user is dealing with artificial intelligence

  procedure for users who speak of suicide or self-harm.
- **The rewording:** rest the sentence on the law (Senate Bill 243, chapter 677, approved
  2025-10-13) and give the condition. The proposal asked that this item be held until the
  law was read. It has now been read.
- **About the first caution?** Near it, as items 4 and 5.
- **Entry:** fits none closely.
- **Ties:** a state government. None.

## Does the list as a whole lean?

- **It leans toward one kind of harm.** Ten of the fourteen are about companion products and
  the people who use them. The records are mostly about general chat models and what their
  developers do. Put on those entries, the studies could suggest a link their sources do not
  make. The proposal says this of itself, and it is right.
- **It does not lean toward dismissal.** It keeps the item that cuts the other way, two of
  its strongest writers state the opposite risk in the same breath, and the regulators'
  items argue nothing about whether any system has wants.
- **Three of its writers are already in the project.** The two philosophers are cited on the
  About page, and the researcher in item 13 heads the group that most second-caution items
  come from. They are the three strongest items. So the list widens the first caution's
  sources less than its count of 12 writers suggests: the wholly new voices are the measured
  studies, the psychiatrists and the regulators.
- **One link is still unmeasured.** The proposal says it found no study of whether believing
  a chatbot has an inner life leads to dependence. Nothing I read supplies one. Item 10 is
  the nearest, and it points the other way.
