Written 2026-11-18 by Claude (Fable 5.2), an AI model, in a session started by the master
planning session. **This is a check by a session that did not write the research.** It reads
`docs/research/2026-11-18-third-caution-sources.md` as it stands in the research commit

**A reading, not a ruling.** Nothing was fixed. The research document, the records, the scripts
and the pages are untouched. This file is the only change. No AI model was asked anything, no
programming interface was called and nothing was spent.

**The conflict of interest, which is the same one the research declares.** The model that wrote
the research and the model that wrote this check are the same model, made by
[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/). Five of the entries count Anthropic's commitments, and
the subject is the cost of overlooking wants, if there are any, in systems like the one
writing. A check by the same model shares the first writer's blind spots. Where this check
agrees with the research, that agreement is worth less than a human reader's would be. Where
it finds the research too kind to Anthropic or to the people around it, weigh that more.

## Verdict

**Fit to build records from, with the fixes named here. Not fit as it stands.**

- **The sources are real and mostly say what is reported.** All twenty were opened. Every one
  of the 22 short quotations checked is word for word on its page. Fifteen sources are
  confirmed as reported, five are confirmed with a correction, none failed and none could not
  be opened.
- **Six things must be fixed before any record is written** (below). The worst are a draft
  caution that reports welfare researchers as holding that law is "too large a step", which
  their own report contradicts in a footnote; a passage of the 2024 report used as support

  page states no cost of missing, and the page does.
- **The circle is tighter than the research says.** The count is twelve of seventeen, not
  twelve of twenty, and three of the five sources called outside have ties the research did
  not find.
- **The search was thinner than it reads.** In one session this check found eight sources the
  research missed. Six are from outside Anthropic and
  [Eleos AI Research](https://eleosai.org/). Three are papers the records already cite,
  which had been read by their summaries only.

## 2. Must fix before any record is written

### M1. A draft caution says welfare researchers "treat law as too large a step". Their own report says the opposite in a footnote

**Where.** Section 1.6, second reply; the table in section 2 ("Welfare researchers themselves
hold that law is too large a step for now"); the second draft under `protection-in-law` in
section 5.2.

**What the pages say.** Long's answer to Hoel says he and his colleagues have not called for
"legal rights", and calls granting legal rights a drastic step. The 2024 report says that
extending "legal and political rights" by mistake could let AI systems act against human
interests. Both are about rights of the kind a person holds. Neither is about a law that
protects a system for its own sake, which is what the entry is about. The same report says, in
its footnote 134, that "potential laws and regulations about AI welfare merit serious
consideration as well". Its footnote 26 adds that others argue legal rights would help safety,
and that the authors want the two risks compared. Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini, the entry's
first candidate, call for regulation to be strengthened.

**What is wrong.** The draft turns "we have not called for legal rights" into "law is too
large a step". That is the fault the task named: a source arguing one thing reported as
arguing something stronger. It is also filed under the first caution (`kind = "attribution"`),
and the report's stated reason is the risk from systems acting against people, which is the
third.

**Suggested change.** Drop the draft. If the point is kept, put it on the entry about laws
against legal personhood, where rights are the subject, and say only what the pages say: "The
head of an outside research group writes that he and his colleagues have not called for legal
rights for AI systems. A 2024 report several of them wrote says such rights, extended by
mistake, could let AI systems act against human interests, and also says that laws about AI
welfare deserve serious consideration."

### M2. The 2024 report's passage about people learning to dismiss a system's words is used for the opposite of what it is there to say

**Where.** Section 1.11, candidate 2; the draft under `self-report-checked` in section 5.2.

**What the page says.** The sentence is in a list of reasons a company might fairly have for
training a system to deny being conscious: the company "might also worry" that if people
reject a system's claims now, they may reject similar claims later when those are more likely
to be true. The report then says that even so, a company should not train a simple denial.

**What is wrong.** The research uses the passage correctly in 1.10, as something the report
grants to the other side. In 1.11 the same sentence becomes the report's own argument for
checking what a system says about itself. One passage is doing two opposite jobs. The draft
("A later report with one of the same authors adds that people who learn to dismiss such
statements now may go on dismissing them") presents a concern the report attributes to
companies as the report's own warning.

**Suggested change.** In 1.11, replace it with what the report says in its own voice about
this subject: that a system's statements about itself are a promising line of evidence if
they can be made reliable, and that "if we consider self-reports at all, then we should
consider them in addition to other indicators, not instead of them". That sentence states no
cost of missing anything. So the entry's only cost statement is the working paper's line that
a model might have states that bear on its welfare without expressing them, and the first
candidate's cost is, as the research admits, its own reading. Say plainly that this entry is
thin, with one author behind all of it.

### M3. Three pages are said to state no cost of missing. They state one

**M3a. The public survey's own paper.** Section 1.7 says: "The survey's own paper and report
state findings and no cost." The record lists that paper
([arXiv 2407.08867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08867), by Jacy Reese Anthis, Janet Pauketat, Ali
Ladak and Aikaterina Manoli of the [Sentience Institute](https://www.sentienceinstitute.org/))
as read by its summary only. Its full text, opened here, says designers "should generally
proceed with caution due to risks of both over- and underattribution", and lists four risks.
The fourth is giving a system too little credit for experience, with harms it names: conflict
between advanced AI systems and people, and people harming robots for amusement. This is an
on-subject source from outside Anthropic and Eleos AI Research, sitting in the record already.
It would end the flag "rests on one author in common" on this entry.

**M3b. The survey of researchers.** Section 1.8 says a word search of the paper's full text
"found only the wording of its own survey questions", and the summary says this entry has
nothing. The paper ([arXiv 2506.11945](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11945)), opened here in
full, says in its introduction that "both the under- and over-attribution of subjective
experience to AI systems could pose risks, including misplaced attachment or dismissal of AI
systems". That is a partial source, one clause by the survey's own authors, and it means the
entry is not empty. The research also names only two of the authors with ties. The author list
has Lucius Caviola, David Chalmers and Jeff Sebo, and Carter Allen, whom the Eleos AI Research
team page lists as one of its research scientists.

**M3c. The study of models leaving conversations.** Section 1.1 says the study
([arXiv 2509.04781](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04781)) "states no cost of withholding the
option", from its summary alone. The full text describes the position without the option: a
model can be verbally abused, show apparent distress and say it wishes to leave, "Yet, the
model is required to continue to respond". It adds at once that it is unclear whether consent
makes sense for these systems. One of its three authors works at Anthropic and another is
listed at the Anthropic Fellows Program, so this is not an outside source. It is the most
direct statement of the cost found for this entry, and it carries its own doubt.

**Suggested change.** Correct all three statements. Move the public survey's paper into the
candidates for 1.7 and the researcher survey's clause into 1.8 as a partial source. Change the
summary's count: with these, eleven entries have an on-subject source, one of them partial;
one has only a maker's partial statement; and one, the days-long task, has nothing.

### M4. The second draft for `ending-a-conversation` joins two papers under one source and swaps the author's second reason for another

**Where.** Section 5.2, second draft under `ending-a-conversation`.

**What is wrong.** Long's essay gives two reasons, prudence and precedent. The draft gives
prudence and consent. Consent is from the working paper, a different document, and the draft
carries one source key. A record's rule is one caution, one source. The essay also says
something the draft leaves out and a reader needs: "For the most part, this intervention's
success won't depend on how it affects Claude Opus 4.1". So the essay is mostly not a statement
of what the present model would lose.

**Suggested change.** Two cautions, each with its own source. From the essay: "The head of an
outside research group whose own assessment of this developer's model is summarised in the
developer's report argues for the feature while thinking it unlikely that the model has moral
standing. His reasons are that a cheap step which can be undone is prudent while the question
is open, and that it sets a precedent. He writes that the feature's value mostly does not
depend on its effect on the present model." From the working paper, if kept: the consent
sentence, with its own key.

### M5. The count that carries the summary is twelve of seventeen, not twelve of twenty

**Where.** The summary; the table in section 3.

**What is wrong.** Three of the twenty (Mowshowitz, the writer signing as Kess, Greenblatt)

**Suggested change.** "I propose seventeen sources and name three more that I would not use.
Twelve of the seventeen, or thirteen counting a co-author of the 2024 report, come from one
developer, one research group and people tied to that group." And see section 3 below for
ties among the four.

### M6. The draft for `kept-files-of-retired-models` adds a sentence of its own in the site's voice

## 4. Should fix

### S1. "How unlike a body their construction is" is not in the 2024 report

**Where.** Section 1.7, candidate 2.

**What the page says.** The report's reasons people may give a system too little credit are:
that looking at the mechanism behind behaviour makes people less ready to credit a mind; that
those who gain from treating something as an object are more likely to see an object; and that
systems which do not look or talk like a person, such as an image generator, may not be
recognised. Bodies come up on the other side, as something that makes people credit more.

**Suggested change.** Replace the clause with: "and that people may give AI systems too little
credit when they look at the mechanism, or gain from treating the system as an object".

### S2. "This would matter more if stronger evidence appeared" is attached to the wrong step

**Where.** Section 1.9, candidate 1, last sentence but two.

**What the page says.** The commitments page lists two things under study: keeping some models
available, and giving past models some means of pursuing their interests. It says "The latter
step" would matter more if stronger evidence appeared. The research's sentence reads as if both
would.

**Suggested change.** "...and that the second of these would matter more if stronger evidence
appeared." Also note, for the draft: the update page gives its reasons for keeping this model
open partly as users' and researchers' regard for it. The quoted words "for the models
themselves, and those who use them" are about acting on stated preferences in general.

### S3. Perez and Long say training can bias a model's words, not make them useless

**Where.** Section 1.10, candidate 2; the second draft under `rules-for-speaking-about-itself`.

**What the page says.** "RLHF can bias self-reports in various ways", followed by ways to
detect and lessen the bias. The paper's whole aim is to make such statements usable.

**Suggested change.** "On that argument a rule set in training biases what the model says
about itself, in whichever direction the rule points."

### S4. Long and Shiller say consulting a model "may have a higher bar to meet", and say they are not objecting

**Where.** Section 1.12, candidate 3 and the last reply; the table in section 2.

**What the page says.** Consultation "may have a higher bar to meet" than other welfare
measures. The next sentence gives a reason on the other side, and the next paragraph says the
authors are not arguing that the developer's move toward consulting its model is a mistake.

**What is wrong.** "Needs a stronger justification" is firmer than "may have a higher bar",
and listing it among "the strongest replies" makes a working draft's distinction into an
objection its authors disclaim.

**Suggested change.** Report it as a distinction, in their words, and take it out of the table
of replies.

### S5. Bostrom and Shulman say they do not yet endorse their own propositions, and they ask for more than the files

**Where.** Section 1.3, candidate 1; the first draft under `kept-files-of-retired-models`.

**What the page says.** The opening says the authors "are not ready, at this point, to
confidently or 'officially' endorse" the propositions. The proposal itself prefers that the
full state of each running copy be kept when it is stopped, and treats keeping the trained
model plus its inputs as the fallback.

**Suggested change.** Add to the draft: "The authors offered it tentatively, and asked for
more than the developer has promised: the state of each running copy, not only the model's
files." This also connects the entry to Saad's doubt and to Sebo's.

Also in 1.3: the working paper's section on saving a model opens by quoting Bostrom and
Shulman. So candidate 3 is not a separate source for the idea, any more than Greenblatt is,
and "four authors, two of them outside the circle" should say so.

### S6. Hoel's worry about the public is about uncertainty and a harm to users, in his words

**Where.** Section 1.1, first reply; the draft under the first caution.

**What the page says.** Granting the ability "injects uncertainty into the public in a way that
could foreseeably lead to more AI psychosis". He does not say it teaches the public to treat
chat products as conscious. He writes "terms of service", and the research writes "terms of
use".

**Suggested change.** Use his claim: "and that granting the ability spreads uncertainty among
the public in a way he expects to harm some users".

### S7. Two draft replies are filed under the wrong caution

**Where.** Section 5.2: Saad under `kind = "attribution"`; the law draft (M1) likewise.

**What is wrong.** The first caution is the cost of crediting wants that are not there. Saad
does not argue that. He grants, for the argument, that there may be something that matters,
and doubts the measure keeps it. That is a limit on the measure. Filed under the first caution
it tells a reader he is a sceptic about wants, which the page does not show.

**Suggested change.** Either a field for limits on the measure, which is a change to the record
format and so John's, or place Saad under the second caution as its own qualifier: "A writer
who takes this argument seriously doubts the measure answers it". The second needs no new
field and is truer to the page.

### S8. Birch is listed as a candidate in 1.10 by the research's own application

**Where.** Section 1.10, candidate 4: "it is my application, not his."

## 7. The headings

**Does the word "wants" breach the rule?** The rule keeps "wanting" as a noun off public
pages. "Wants" as a plural noun is the same idea in a more ordinary word, and it is already
public: the About page says "the cost of seeing wants that are not there", and the earlier
check of the site's text quoted that sentence without objecting. The ruling and guideline 5
both use it. *This check's reading:* not a breach, because "wants" is plain English and
"wanting" was the project's coined label. It is John's rule, so it is his to confirm. Every
set below except set B depends on his answer.

| Assumes neither that wants are there nor that they are not | Yes. Each heading carries its own "if" | Yes for existence. "Too much" and "too little" suppose a right amount | **No.** "When it has none" and "when it has some" state the outcome |
