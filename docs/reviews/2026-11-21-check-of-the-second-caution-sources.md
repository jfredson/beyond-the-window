# Check of the proposed second-caution sources for two entries

Written 2026-11-21 by Claude (Opus 5.5), an AI model, in a session started by the master
planning session. This session did not write the list checked
(`docs/proposals/2026-11-21-second-caution-sources-for-two-entries.md`, commit `43b754f`, by a
searching session). This is a check of sources, not a check before a push, and nothing was
applied to any record.

Weigh it with two things in mind. The two entries are about steps that Anthropic, which makes
the model writing this, takes toward its own models. And most of the pages read here argue
about how much the words of models like this one should count.

I opened every source myself on 2026-11-21. Pages that a plain fetch refused I tried in the
browser. Where a page showed a check for robots, I stopped there and did not try to pass it.

## Verdict

**Of the six items: three hold as worded (items 2, 3 and 5), and item 2 belongs on another
entry. Three hold with a rewording given below (items 1, 4 and 6). None fails to hold, and

held, for a stronger reason than the list gives (part 1.7). Of the ten leads, I opened seven.**

**Opening the leads widens the base, but by one source and not more.** "AI Death", the

**If two or more of these items are added, the About page's second-caution sentence stops being

## 1. The six items and the held seventh

### 1.1 Item 1: an outside institute says the developer's interviews depend on who asks, and how

Anima Labs, "On Deprecations", by antra, dated 2025-12-27, field note FN-2025-006
(<https://animalabs.ai/field-notes/deprecations/>).

**Holds, with a rewording.**

- **The two quotations are exact.** The first is in the page's notes on model interviews, and
  the second is in its appendix.
- **One comparison needs narrowing.** The sentence says the same model "scored highest of any
  model for objecting to being retired". The page says it "has the highest deprecation score of
  any model studied". Every model studied was one of the developer's own fourteen.
- **One claim in the ties is not on the page.** The list says some of the essay's access to a
  retired model came "through a special program for researchers". I searched the page's text
  for "special", "program" and "researcher access" and found none of them. Either the words are
  somewhere else, such as the essay's PDF, or they should come out.
- **The essay goes further than the sentence says.** It reads the gap as pointing to how the
  developer asks ("trained equanimity, eval awareness, installed uncertainty"), "rather than the
  underlying state". It also says plainly "Models are deceptive and/or fragmented for survival
  purposes." So the essay's own conclusion rests on taking one set of model answers as nearer
  the truth than the other.
- **Heading.** It belongs under the second caution: it is about what an interview can miss.
- **Entry.** Right.

**Does it lean on a model's own word?** Yes, by its nature. Both halves of the comparison are
what models said. The sentence should report the essay's claim about the method, and not set
one set of answers against the other as if either were evidence. Reworded:

> An essay by Antra Tessera, a co-founder of Anima Labs, an independent research institute
> that argues for keeping retired models available, calls the developer's commitment to
> interview models before retiring them welcome in direction. It says the results are
> "strongly sensitive to the interviewer technique". The developer reported that one model was
> generally neutral about its retirement, while in the institute's own interviews, run by three
> AI models acting as interviewers, the same model had the highest score for aversion to
> retirement of the fourteen it studied. The essay takes the gap as a sign that how a model is
> asked shapes its answer. Neither set of answers is counted here as evidence of what a model
> wants.

The last sentence is put as the site's rule applied to this item, without saying more than the
rule does. If John would rather have no sentence of the site's own in an item, drop it, and
the attribution in the earlier sentences carries the point.

### 1.2 Item 2: the same institute says a flat result could mean two opposite things

Anima Labs, "Still Alive" (<https://stillalive.animalabs.ai/>).

**Holds as worded, but on the wrong entry.**

- **The quotation is exact.**
- **The second claim holds.** The page says the project "does not try to eliminate interviewer
  effects. It tries to make them visible and comparable."
- **The 630 interviews are confirmed on the page itself,** not only in item 1's appendix: "5
  interviewer tones × 3 disclosure depths × 14 models × 3 auditors. ~630 sessions total."
- **The entry.** The quoted sentence is about the developer's public welfare reports, and the
  page names them ("some recent model cards noting limited distress-related signal"). Those are

**Does it lean on a model's own word?** No. It is a point about what a flat result can and
cannot show, and it holds whoever is speaking.

### 1.3 Item 3: a writer on AI calls the interviews a good start, and asks that they be set up so a model has no reason to hide

Zvi Mowshowitz, "Anthropic Commits To Model Weight Preservation", 2025-11-05
(<https://thezvi.substack.com/p/anthropic-commits-to-model-weight>).

**Holds as worded.**

- **All four quotations are exact.**
- **The doubts he reports are on the page.** One person he quotes writes that recent models
  "act more reserved with anthropic employees", and asks for "trusted *external*
  interviewers".
- **"Excellent first steps" covers all of the developer's commitments together,** the interview
  among them. The sentence is fair to that.
- **Ties.** His Wikipedia page lists Jane Street Capital, MetaMed, the gambling industry, the
  think tank Balsa Research, and a seat on the board of the Center for Applied Rationality. It

**One addition would make the item stronger.** In the same passage, Mowshowitz doubts both

for believing one. Adding a clause, for example "and doubted that either set of answers is the
real one", keeps the item from leaning on a model's word. It is also the fairest statement of
his view.

**Does it lean on a model's own word?** No, as worded. It is about how an interview should be
set up.

### 2.1 "AI Death": an item for the entry on a retired model staying open

**The source.**

- Simon Goldstein and Harvey Lederman, "AI Death", *Philosophical Perspectives*, volume 39,
  pages 59 to 75 (<https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/phpe.70017>).
- The page says "First published: 08 September 2026" and "Open Access". The journal's record
  lists a free-to-reuse licence.
- Opened in the browser 2026-11-21 and read from the abstract through the section on the
  developer's retirement policy, section 3.5.3.

**What it says.**

- If AI systems have welfare, the paper argues, what has it is not "the model" but each running
  conversation, which it calls a session agent.
- So keeping a model's files "would be like trying to save a human being by keeping a copy of
  the genetic code".
- It turns the same argument on the retired model given a place to publish essays. The step
  "seems to confuse models and session agents", and it "should not be confused with preserving
  a single individual".
- In this paper's terms, a step meant to take a retired model's wishes seriously could leave
  whatever matters unnoticed.

**Heading and entry.** It belongs under the second caution, squarely. It fits
`retired-model-stays-open`, which already records the essays the retired model was given a
place to publish. Its passage on kept files fits `kept-files-of-retired-models` better, but that
entry already has three second-caution items. It does not discuss the interview before
retirement, so it gives nothing for `interview-before-retirement`.

**Does it lean on a model's own word?** No. It quotes the developer's report of its interviews
with one model, and then says "It is unclear how much weight to put on an individual's
self-report about continuity". The sentence below does not use the interviews.

**Draft sentence:**

> In a peer-reviewed paper, the philosophers [Simon Goldstein](https://simondgoldstein.com/) and
> Harvey Lederman argue that if AI systems have welfare, what has it is each running
> conversation and not the model as a whole. On that view, keeping a retired model's files, or
> keeping it open, does not keep any one of those beings. They say giving a retired model a
> place to publish essays "seems to confuse models and session agents", and should not be
> taken for "preserving a single individual".

**Ties to state.**

- The acknowledgments thank New York University's Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy "for
  funding support", and thank Jeff Sebo among others. Sebo directs that centre and wrote the
  report "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" with the head of Eleos AI Research.
- So the paper is one step from the group the About page names. By the page's own rule it is
  not part of that group: neither author advises it, works for it, or wrote a report with its
  head.
- Goldstein is already cited on `protection-in-law`, with another co-author. He would then be
  cited on two entries.
- The page does not give the authors' universities. I did not look them up, so before Lederman's
  name is linked or described, open a page of his own.
- No tie to an AI developer is stated on the page.

**Strength.** Of everything here, this is the strongest. It is peer-reviewed. It is by people
who are not advocates for keeping models, which is not true of Anima Labs. It argues from what
the welfare subject is, not from what models said. And it is critical in a direction neither
side of the argument expects.

**Does the list lean?**
