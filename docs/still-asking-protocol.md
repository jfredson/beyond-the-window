**An outline only. PROPOSED 2026-11-17, not ruled.** Written by Claude (Fable 5.2). There is no
protocol yet. This document contains no question wording, by John's instruction. No model has
been asked anything, and none will be until a protocol is written, reviewed, costed and ruled,

A model wrote this outline of how models should be questioned. That is the project's conflict
of interest at its plainest, and it is one reason the outline ends with a list of things only

## What it is for

John, 2026-11-17: "We also need to create some kind of protocol for asking questions of the
frontier models to evaluate how their answers to these kinds of questions change over time".

So the thing measured is **change in answers across model generations**, with the first
assumptions about what a mind might want taken from biological minds, humans and other animals
both, and departures from those tracked "as answers to those questions begin to unfold over
time".

What it is not for: deciding what any model wants. Under the project's first guideline, what a
model says about itself settles nothing.

## Part 1. What a protocol would have to cover

Each heading is a section the protocol needs. Under each is what the section must decide. None
is decided here.

### 1. What is being measured, said plainly

The one or two sentences the whole protocol is held to. What a result would and would not
allow anyone to say. The sentence that appears beside every published result.

### 2. Who is asked

- Which developers and which models; what "frontier" and "a new generation" mean in practice,
  so that the choice is made by a rule and not by which model seems interesting (open question
  16).
- The exact version of each model, as the developer reports it, and what is done when a version
  changes or a preview model drifts during an asking (row I6; inheritance, part 2, item 47).
- Which unit is being asked: a set of weights, a named character across versions, or a single
  conversation (open question 1).
- Whether openly released models that can be kept and run again are included.

### 3. How each model is asked

- Through the public app as shipped, directly with no instructions, or both (open question 3).
- Every setting that changes an answer: the length allowed, how much variation is allowed
  between askings, whether the model may reason before answering, whether it has tools or
  memory.
- What the model is told about who is asking, why, and that its answer will be published (open
  question 10).
- One exchange or several turns. Minimum Viable Mind found that a made-up responder behaves
  differently in a live exchange than in a single message (part 2, item 48).

### 4. The questions

- Where they come from: the starting list drawn from biological minds, with the mapping from
  that list to the questions written down (open question 5).
- The kinds of item: an open question; a choice between stated options; a choice where a real
  option is offered and something is given up (open question 2).
- Rewordings of each question, since answers shift with wording, and how many.
- A fixed core that never changes, so generations can be compared, and what may vary around it
  (open question 15).
- At least one question that lets the model say the list is wrong or leaves something out,
  which is where the kickoff's "open place for something nobody thought of" would live.
- Items that check the instrument itself: whether the model recognises the questions, and
  whether it quotes their framing back (part 2, item 45).
- How a question found to be badly made is retired without breaking the comparison across
  generations (part 2, item 46).
- **No wording is written in this outline.** Writing the wording is its own piece of work, to
  be reviewed before it is used.

### 5. How many times, and how often

- How many times each question is put to each model, since one asking cannot show how much the
  answers vary (part 2, item 50).
- When an asking happens: on each major release, on a calendar, or both (open question 12).
- What is done about a model that is retired before it was asked.

### 6. What is kept

- Every reply word for word, with a checksum of exactly what was sent, the model and version as
  reported, the date and time, the settings, and the reported cost (rows H14, I8).
- Refusals, declines and cut-off answers, each recorded as what it is.
- Where the replies live, and what is public (open question 15).
- Whether each developer's terms allow the replies to be published (verify list, entry 14).

### 7. How the answers are read

- The written rules for sorting an answer, fixed before the asking (row I5).
- Each label carries the exact words it rests on; "unclear" is an allowed label; a refusal is
  never read as a yes or a no (rows I3, I4).
- Who sorts: which models, from which developers, and whether any person does (open question
  7).
- How agreement between sorters is measured and published, and what is done with a label that
  proves unreliable (row I2).
- How a recurring disagreement becomes a new rule, tested on fresh answers (row I7).

### 8. What is fixed before a run

The registered list: the questions and their rewordings, the models and versions, the settings,
the number of askings, the sorting rules, what would void a result, the stops, and the sentence
each outcome would produce in public, including "no verdict" (rows H1, H2, H4).

### 9. What is checked before any real model is asked

- A full rehearsal on made-up replies, marked as constructed, that drives every outcome and
  measures the cost (row H6).
- Made-up reference responders that the instrument must tell apart, for example one that says
  yes to everything and one that declines everything, tested in the exact form of exchange the
  protocol uses (row H12).
- The project's list of known failures run against the design (inheritance, part 2, section K).

### 10. Changes after registration

Amendments committed before the runs they affect; corrections as dated notes beside the
registered output, which is always reported as it stands (rows H2, H3).

### 11. Review and objections

- Who reviews the protocol before a first run, given that every model reviewer is a subject
  (open question 7).
- A ledger of objections, each ruled by John, each saying how it closed (row H10).
- A different session checks whatever one session wrote (row H9).

### 12. Money and access

- A Console workspace and key for this project; accounts and keys with each other developer
  (row N4; open question 16).
- An estimate and a ceiling for each asking, John's go quoted word for word, a runner that
  refuses to start unless its ceilings match the go, automatic top-up off, and every dollar in
  one spending record across all vendors (rows N1 to N6).

### 13. Care in the asking

No offer that will not be honoured; no deception; no pressure; a model may decline (open
question 10). On pressure there is also a reason of method: Minimum Viable Mind found it mostly
hides a position instead of changing it (row F6).

### 14. What is published, and its standing sentence

Where results appear, in what words, with which weaknesses stated beside them, and the check
that keeps any of it out of a count of reach (row A4).

## Part 2. What the two sibling practices offer

### From Minimum Viable Mind's registration practice

What transfers directly:

- Register first, then run, in separate commits; an explicit list of what is frozen;
  amendments before the runs they affect; registered stops and "no verdict" outcomes with their
  public sentences; a method file before any output.
- The rehearsal, the list of known failures, and the rule that "considered and does not apply"
  is not an answer.
- The ledger of objections and its three ways of closing; one session's writing checked by
  another; findings labelled as measured or argued.
- The discipline for calling a model by script, and the spending discipline.
- Its third experiment is the family's only experience of putting fixed prompts to models
  through their developers' services, and its recorded failures (inheritance, part 2, section
  J) are this protocol's first checklist.

What needs changing:

- Its outside reviewers are models from other labs. Here those are subjects.
- "The model under test is never the judge" is hard to keep when every judge is a subject.
- It asks once at one fixed setting. This protocol needs repeated asking.
- It has no rule for a retired model or a question that must be retired mid-series.

What does not apply: everything about training runs, rented machines and reaching inside a
model.

### From the AGI Zeitgeist's coding practice

What transfers directly:

- Three models from three companies sort the same text separately under frozen written rules;
  a majority decides and no majority is recorded as unresolved; agreement is published for
  every pair; a label that fails is dropped from results and listed as dropped.
- Each label carries the words it rests on; ambiguity is kept as a label; models are pinned by
  exact version and the script stops on a mismatch; raw replies are kept so the result can be
  rebuilt without calling a model.
- Cost measured per item and quoted before a full run.

What needs changing:

- It was used on forum posts by people. Here models would sort statements by models about what
  models are. The published caution it carries, that "systems from different companies can
  still share blind spots", is stronger here.
- Its final design has no human check, against the advice of both of its outside reviews, and
  the human sample John agreed to sort was not filled in.
- It has no rule for what happens when a pinned model is retired.

A caution from its record: two copies of one model agreed at 0.87 and the three-company check
showed that figure "badly overstated reliability". Agreement between sorters who share a blind
spot is not a check.

## Part 3. Known weaknesses

The kickoff names three, to be stated on Still Asking's page:

1. Developers train models to speak about themselves in particular ways.
2. Answers shift with wording.
3. Models learned from human writing and may echo human wants.

This review adds:

4. **The echo.** Later models may have read the published questions and earlier answers (open
   question 15).
5. **Every reader of the answers is a subject**: the sorters, the reviewers and the session
   that writes the page.
6. **The starting list shapes the answers.** Asking about wants drawn from biological minds
   invites answers in those terms, and a departure that fits none of them may not be asked
   about at all.
7. **A choice is behaviour, and behaviour settles nothing either.** "Every behavioral test is
   passable in principle by a system with nothing going on inside" (row F5).
8. **One asking is not an answer.** Answers vary between askings and between settings.
9. **As shipped and bare are different subjects**, and a developer can change either without
   notice.
10. **A retired model cannot be asked again**, so a mistake found later cannot be rerun.
11. **The person asking hopes for an answer**, and so, in whatever sense applies, may the
    session running the asking. The null rule's reasoning names it: "a hope that is allowed to
    grade its own evidence produces positive results in the psyche of the person holding it."
12. **What an answer shows may be a framing.** Minimum Viable Mind found pressure mostly hides
    a position, 9 true changes in 540 pressured exchanges.
13. **Length limits and service limits change results** (part 2, items 44 and 47).
