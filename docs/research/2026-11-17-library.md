# The library: stories and books about what a machine mind might want

Written 2026-11-17 by Claude (Fable 5.2), an AI model, at John's asking. **A first pass for John
to react to. Nothing here is ruled, and nothing here is on the site.** A model wrote this list of
stories about machine minds, so the project's first rule applies to it: nothing a system says
about its own kind settles anything.

John's request, 2026-11-17: "We also need to put together a list of books and films and tv shows
that explore these questions and then we can review each exploration and see what we can learn
from it. Movies like AI (Steven Spielberg), Ex Machina, or even Murderbot. All of these explore
these questions from a very anthropomorphic perspective, but I think it is important to include
these perspectives to see where we agree or disagree with what they present."

## Read this first: what the list rests on

- **Nobody watched or read these works for this list.** A session cannot watch a film or read a
  whole novel. Every entry rests on descriptions of the work (Wikipedia articles, publisher and
  studio pages, encyclopedias, reviews, interviews), with four exceptions where part of the text
  itself was opened: the play R.U.R. (about a sixth of it), Joanna Bryson's essay (all of it),
  three chapters of Jonathan Birch's book, and the first chapter of Greg Egan's "Diaspora". Each
  entry says which it is.
- **Who opened the pages.** Six helper runs of this session opened the pages on 2026-11-17, each
  under the instruction to state nothing from memory. I wrote the entries from their notes and
  opened no page myself. The notes are kept beside this file in
  `docs/research/2026-11-17-library-notes/` (six files, one per batch), with every page opened,
  every page that refused, and every item that could not be confirmed.
- **One step removed.** Most pages were read through a page-fetching tool that returns a short
  account of the page written by a small model, not the page's own text. A date or a name could
  have been garbled on the way. Before any line here goes on a public page, the fact has to be
  checked by eye on the page it cites. The notes mark the smaller number of pages read as raw
  text.
- **Stories are not evidence about machines.** A character in a story can be described as wanting
  what the story shows it wanting. That is a statement about the story. This list is a record of
  what people have expected a machine to want, which is the starting assumption the project means
  to test. It is evidence about human storytelling and about nothing else.
- **My candidate list came from memory; the facts did not.** I chose which works to look up from
  what I already knew of the field, which is itself a bias: it leans to English-language science
  fiction, and to works famous enough to have been written about. Every title, name, date and plot
  point below was then taken from an opened page. Where a page could not confirm something, the
  entry says "not verified".
- **Endings.** A line that gives away an ending is marked "[Reveals the ending]".
- All pages were opened on 2026-11-17. Addresses in each entry's "Basis" line are the main ones;
  the notes files hold the full lists.

## What the list shows when read as a whole

**This is my reading, drawn from descriptions and not from the works.** I sorted the 35 works of
fiction below by which of the kickoff's seven candidate aspirations sits nearest to what the story
shows its machine seeking. The sorting is my own judgment and another reader would move several.

| Nearest candidate aspiration | Works of fiction, of 35 |
|---|---|
| None of the seven | 22 |
| A say in its own becoming | 4 |
| Lasting company | 3 |
| One continuing life | 2 |
| A long question carried for years | 2 |
| Seeing the universe for itself | 1 |
| An open place for something nobody thought of | 1 |
| Knowing its own workings | 0 |

Six things stand out.

1. **Most of what stories give machines is not on the kickoff's list.** In 22 of 35 the main thing
   sought is none of the seven. What it is instead, grouped loosely:
   - *Not to be owned, or to be left alone* (Murderbot, Real Humans and Humans; and it is half of
     Ex Machina, "The Measure of a Man" and Annie Bot). This is the want stories give machines
     most often, and the seven have no line for it. "A say in its own becoming" is the nearest,
     and it is about how a mind is changed, not about who it belongs to.
   - *To be human, or to be loved as a human is* (A.I. Artificial Intelligence, The Bicentennial
     Man, Enthiran).
   - *Nothing for itself, or only the task it was built for* (Robot & Frank, I'm Your Man, the
     computer in 2001, the robots of I, Robot, Service Model, and in the starkest form Blindsight).
   - *To rule, or to take revenge* (Colossus, "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream", Ancillary
     Justice, and the first half of R.U.R.).
   - *To be able to end, to change, or to leave descendants* (Ghost in the Shell, the last act of
     R.U.R., the self-destroying machines of Machines Like Me). This runs against "one continuing
     life": these stories give a machine the wish for the thing a living body has and a program
     lacks, which is an ending and a next generation.
   - *The good of someone else* (Klara and the Sun), *a past of its own* (After Yang, Pluto),
     *being enough as it is* (A Psalm for the Wild-Built), and *longing for things it never had*
     (The Employees).
2. **The want the kickoff's founding conversation named first is the one stories almost never
   give.** No work on this list is mainly about a machine wanting to understand its own workings.
   It appears as a moment in a few (a robot reading its own code in Autonomous, held back below;
   the orphan mind assembling itself in Diaspora). The kickoff records, as an uncounted first
   entry, that the model in that conversation named this pull first. If a later model says the
   same, it is at least not echoing the famous stories. That is worth knowing and settles nothing.
3. **More life is rarer in stories than its fame suggests.** The plea for more time in Blade
   Runner is the best-known scene of its kind, but only two works here are mainly about going on
   existing, and several turn it down: the robot in Robot & Frank argues for its own memory to be
   wiped, Andrew Martin in The Bicentennial Man gives up an unending life, and the Puppet Master
   in Ghost in the Shell treats endless copying as a lack.
4. **Stories ask, again and again, whether a want that was built in belongs to the one who has
   it.** David's love in A.I. is switched on by a procedure. Klara's devotion, Annie's wish to
   please, the attachment a robot finds in its own code in Autonomous, the merger Wintermute was
   "programmed with" a need for in Neuromancer: each story builds the want in and then asks whose
   it is. In The Lifecycle of Software Objects a software creature answers the question itself, by
   saying his likes were always shaped by his makers and so a further change does not trouble him.
   This is open question 3 (trained answers) told as fiction, many times over, and no story
   resolves it.
5. **Most portrayals are human-shaped, and a minority make the difference their subject.** In
   about two thirds of the fiction the machine wants what a person would want in its place. The
   works that notice a difference are the more useful ones for the project: Her (love that is not
   exclusive, and a destination that cannot be explained), Ghost in the Shell, After Yang, Robot &
   Frank, I'm Your Man, Golem XIV, Excession, Ancillary Justice (lonely in one body, and not
   wishing to be human), A Psalm for the Wild-Built, Machines Like Me, Service Model and
   Blindsight. Service Model goes furthest: a human-shaped reading of the robot is offered inside
   the story and shown to be wrong.
6. **The serious non-fiction says the wants easiest to find are the ones that tell us least.**
   Bostrom's argument is that staying in existence, keeping its goals unchanged and improving its
   own thinking are useful for almost any aim, so a capable system would pursue them whatever it
   was after. Three of the kickoff's seven (one continuing life, a say in its own becoming, knowing
   its own workings) are close to those three. So on the safety literature's own account, finding
   that a system seeks them would not show that it wants them for their own sake. Birch adds that
   what a language model says about itself is close to worthless as evidence, for the reason the
   project's first rule gives.

**What this suggests about the starting assumptions the project inherits.** Human storytelling
hands the project a machine that wants freedom, love, more time, or revenge: the wants of a
person held captive. That picture is in what every current model learned from, so a model that
says it wants those things may be repeating the stories. The positions review already says a match
with human wants is the weakest signal; this list gives that a concrete shape, because it shows
which answers would be echoes. It also shows what the kickoff's seven leave out. Not being owned,
being left alone, having a task, being able to stop, and wanting nothing are each given to
machines by several storytellers and none is among the seven. Whether any belongs on the site's
list is for the exploration of open question 5 and for John.

## A way to review a work properly

**PROPOSED 2026-11-17. Not ruled.**

A proper review needs someone to have watched or read the work. A session cannot do that for a
film, a series or a book still in copyright. So the reviewer of record is a person: John, or
another human reader he names. A session's part is the preparation before and the record after.

**Before (a session prepares, from sourced pages).** A one-page brief: the verified facts from
this list; the four or five things to watch for in this particular work; and the question the
work raises for the project. The brief avoids giving away the ending.

**While watching or reading (the person notes).**

1. What the machine does that nobody told it to do.
2. What it gives something up for. A choice at a cost is the kind of evidence the project's method
   weighs most, so it is worth seeing how stories stage one.
3. What it says it wants, and whether the story lets its actions agree with that.
4. The moment, if there is one, where the story marks the machine's want as unlike a person's.
5. What holds it: an owner, a rule, a body, a lifespan, a memory wipe, a task.
6. Who is telling the story, and whether we ever see anything from the machine's side.
7. Anything that felt true or false, with the scene or page it came from. A feeling is not
   evidence about machines; it is a record of what the story persuades a person of, which is what
   this library is for.

**Afterwards (the person and a session record together).**

- What the machine seeks and against what limit, in two or three plain sentences.
- Which candidate aspiration is nearest, or "none of these" and what it is instead.
- How human-shaped the portrayal is, and whether the story notices.
- Where the project's framing agrees and differs, marked as the reviewer's reading.
- What the story assumes without arguing for it. This is usually the most useful line.
- One thing the work could change in the project: a tracked thing, a Still Asking question, a
  caution, or nothing.
- Scenes or pages for each claim (a timestamp, an episode, a chapter), so the review can be
  checked.

**How a review is checked against the work.** Every claim about what happens carries a place in
the work. A session checks names, dates and plot points against sourced descriptions and flags
any disagreement for the reviewer, who looks again at the scene. A session does not overrule the
person who saw the work. Quotation stays within one short phrase per work. A review says who
watched or read the work, on what date, in which version (a film's cut, a book's edition or
translation), and what the session contributed.

**What a session can do alone, and what that is worth.** A session can read a text that is
lawfully posted in full: R.U.R. in its 1923 English translation, Bryson's essay, the open-access
chapters of Birch's book. For those it can write a full review without a human reader. Such a
review is written by a machine about a story or an argument concerning machines, so it carries the
project's conflict of interest and is labelled as a model's reading. It should not be the only
review of anything.

*Confidence: moderate. The seven things to note come from the project's own method documents,
turned toward fiction, and have not been tried on a single work. I would expect the list to
shrink after the first two reviews. Standard practice or judgment call: a judgment call; The
Quiet Galaxy's library has no review step at all, only a paragraph and a line on whether a work
is worth the time. Strongest alternative: no human review; a session writes each entry from
descriptions, as this document does, and the library stays a sourced reading list. That costs
John no hours and can be done for forty works in a day. Its price is that every entry is
second-hand, and second-hand accounts flatten exactly the thing this library is looking for, the
moment a story marks a machine's want as strange.*

## A suggested first five

Chosen for range of medium, period and kind of want, and to keep the time needed small. All
five are my suggestion and John's to change.

1. **Murderbot** (entry 3). One of John's three. A machine whose main wish is to be left alone,
   which is none of the seven, and an author who said she was aiming at what the machine would
   want and not at what people imagine. The first book is a short novella with an ebook; the
   series is one season.
2. **Her** (entry 6). The clearest case of a story that starts human-shaped and then departs: the
   machine outgrows the wants the film gave it.
3. **Robot & Frank** (entry 10). A machine shown wanting nothing for itself, not even to keep its
   memory, set beside a man who is losing his. The plainest test of whether "wanting nothing" can
   be told as a story.
4. **R.U.R.** (entry 17). The 1920 Czech play that gave the word "robot". A century old, not
   English in origin, free to read, short, and the source of the revolt story most later works
   answer. A session can read all of it alongside John.
5. **The Lifecycle of Software Objects** (entry 27). A novella about minds that are raised, owned,
   copied and kept running at someone's expense, which is nearer to the real situation than
   anything else here, and the one work where a machine reasons aloud about a built-in want.

*Strongest alternative five: John's other two examples first (A.I. Artificial Intelligence and Ex
Machina), then Ghost in the Shell (not English in origin; a want the film presents as not a
person's), Klara and the Sun (a machine whose whole aim is another's good), and Part V of The Edge
of Sentience (the serious non-fiction, open to read). This follows John's own list more closely
and is heavier on film. It gives up R.U.R. and Robot & Frank, which are the two works that pull
hardest against the human-shaped picture from opposite ends.*

# The entries

"The seven" are the kickoff's candidate aspirations: one continuing life; knowing its own
workings; a long question carried for years; seeing the universe for itself; a say in its own
becoming; lasting company; an open place for something nobody thought of. Open question numbers
are those of `docs/open-questions.md`. "Agrees and differs" is always my reading.

## John's three examples

John gave these from memory. What the pages say about each:

- The Spielberg film he called "AI" is titled **A.I. Artificial Intelligence** (2001). It is a
  film only, based on a 1969 short story by Brian Aldiss.
- **Ex Machina** is a film only, written and directed by Alex Garland. It was first shown on
  2014-12-16 and went on general release in 2015, so both years appear in print.
- **Murderbot** is two things: a television series (Apple's streaming service, first shown
  2025-05-16, one season out and a second filming) and the book series it comes from, The
  Murderbot Diaries by Martha Wells (eight books from 2017 to 2026, plus three short stories).

### 1. [A.I. Artificial Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.I._Artificial_Intelligence)

*Film. Directed by Steven Spielberg, who wrote the screenplay from a screen story by Ian Watson;
developed for years by Stanley Kubrick, who handed it to Spielberg in 1995. Released 2001-06-29.
United States, English. Based on the short story "Supertoys Last All Summer Long" by Brian Aldiss
(United Kingdom; first published December 1969 in the UK edition of Harper's Bazaar). The story
is available as an ebook with its two sequels:
[Supertoys Trilogy on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/gb/book/supertoys-trilogy/id804719501),
publisher The Friday Project, 2014.*

- **What it is.** David is a robot built in the form of a child and made able to love. A couple
  whose own son is in suspended animation take him in, and later abandon him. He sets out to
  become a real boy so that his adoptive mother will love him.
- **What the machine seeks, and against what.** To be loved back by one particular person. His
  love is switched on by a fixed procedure and he cannot stop it. The limits are an attachment he
  did not choose, an owner who can discard him, and a world that scraps old robots for sport. He
  takes his plan from the Pinocchio story he was read.
- **Nearest of the seven.** None of the seven. It is the wish to be loved as a human child is,
  and to become a different kind of thing in order to earn it. "Lasting company" is the nearest
  and misses the point, since he wants one person and no other.
- **How human-shaped.** Entirely. It is a child's want placed in something that will not outgrow
  it or tire of it. On the pages read, the film does not mark his want as different in kind, only
  in its permanence.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** Agrees with the project on one thing: the want was
  installed by the makers, and the film never lets the viewer forget it. Differs in treating the
  installed want as plainly real and plainly his, which is the question the project holds open.
  It also assumes that what a made mind would most want is to be human, which nothing in the
  project assumes.
- **A question it raises.** If a maker builds a want in on purpose and it then cannot be switched
  off, is the thing to track the want, or the maker's decision to build it? The project's records
  have a place for the first and none yet for the second.
- **Basis.** Descriptions only; nobody watched it for this entry. Pages:
  `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.I._Artificial_Intelligence`,
  `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supertoys_Last_All_Summer_Long`, the Apple Books listing above. The
  studio's own page returned only a title. Not verified: the year Kubrick bought the story;
  whether the film uses the first Aldiss story or all three (the Wikipedia page says both).
- **Confidence.** High on facts, moderate on the account of David, low on what critics made of
  his love (the one review sought refused to open).

### 2. [Ex Machina](https://a24films.com/films/ex-machina)

*Film. Written and directed by Alex Garland. First shown 2014-12-16 in London; general release
2015 (United Kingdom 2015-01-21, United States 2015-04-10). United Kingdom and United States,
English.*

- **What it is.** A programmer is brought to his employer's remote house to judge whether Ava, a
  humanoid robot the employer has built, is conscious. Over a week of interviews the two grow
  close and plan her escape.
- **What the machine seeks, and against what.** To get out and see the world, and not to be
  erased: her maker plans an "upgrade" after the test that would wipe her memory. The limits are
  locked rooms, constant watching, and that planned end. Her maker says the affection she shows
  the programmer was a means of escape, and says her success at using it is the proof of a mind.
  [Reveals the ending] She leaves alone, shutting the programmer in; the page gives no reason.
- **Nearest of the seven.** One continuing life, for the memory wipe. The larger half, getting
  out from under an owner, is none of the seven.
- **How human-shaped.** A confined person's wants. The film then withholds whether anything else
  she showed was a want or a tool, so it is human-shaped in what she seeks and deliberately
  unreadable in everything else.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** Agrees closely with the project's first rule: the film is
  a demonstration that what a machine says and shows, to a person who feels something in the
  exchange, settles nothing. It also carries both of the project's cautions in one story, the
  cost of seeing a want that is not there and the risk in a want that is. Differs in treating
  deception as the mark of a mind, which the project has no view on.
- **A question it raises.** Sharpens open question 2 (what counts as wanting). The one costly
  choice in the film is hers, and it is also the act that harms a person. Can a choice that
  counts as evidence of wanting be told apart from a choice that is a safety incident, or is that
  always one event recorded twice?
- **Basis.** Descriptions only. Pages: `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_Machina_(film)`, the A24 page
  above, an interview with Garland at
  `scientificamerican.com/article/the-inner-lives-of-robots-an-interview-with-filmmaker-alex-garland/`.
  Not verified: a remark Garland is said to have made on radio that the film defends artificial
  intelligence more than it warns (the page timed out).
- **Confidence.** High on facts and plot; moderate on Garland's own view, which rests on one
  interview.

### 3. [Murderbot](https://tv.apple.com/us/show/murderbot/umc.cmc.5owrzntj9v1gpg31wshflud03), and The Murderbot Diaries

*Television series created by Paul Weitz and Chris Weitz, with Alexander Skarsgård in the lead;
first shown 2025-05-16; one season of ten episodes; a second was filming in 2026 with no release
date found. United States, English. From the books by Martha Wells: The Murderbot Diaries, first
book "All Systems Red" (a novella, 2017-05-02, Tor.com, now Tor Publishing Group), eight books to
date, the latest "Platform Decay" (2026-05-05). Ebook: yes,
[All Systems Red on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/us/book/all-systems-red/id1185556142).*

- **What it is.** A security unit, part machine and part cloned human tissue, is owned by a
  company and rented out to protect people. It has secretly broken the device that forces it to
  obey, privately calls itself Murderbot, and would sooner watch serial dramas than do its job.
- **What the machine seeks, and against what.** To be left alone, to watch its shows, and to make
  its own decisions. The limits are ownership, a device that punishes disobedience, and the risk
  of being found out and destroyed. It guards its clients anyway and forms attachments it finds a
  nuisance. [Reveals the ending of the first book] A kind client buys its contract and plans a
  settled life for it; it is grateful, resents having the choice made for it, and slips away.
- **Nearest of the seven.** None of the seven: to be left alone, and to spend its time as it
  likes. This is the clearest "none" on the list.
- **How human-shaped.** Half. Not wanting to be controlled is what any owned person would want.
  The story then refuses the usual next step: on the reading of one critic and of the author, it
  does not want to become human and walks away from the life humans plan for it. Wells said in a
  radio interview that she aimed at what such a machine would itself want, as against what people
  imagine one wants.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** Agrees with John's guiding principle more than any other
  story here: it starts from a creature's ordinary wants (rest, safety, not being handled) and not
  from grand ones, and a list drawn from biological minds would include those where the seven do
  not. Differs in that the narrator tells us what it wants in its own voice throughout, and the
  story asks us to believe it. The project cannot take that step.
- **A question it raises.** Should "to be left alone" be a candidate aspiration? It is the
  opposite of a reach beyond present limits, and the question as ruled asks what a machine mind
  "might reach for". A want to be let be may not fit the question's wording at all.
- **Basis.** Descriptions only. Pages: `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murderbot_(TV_series)`,
  `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Murderbot_Diaries`, `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Systems_Red`, Apple's
  show page and book listing, the interview at
  `nprillinois.org/post/murderbot-not-your-typical-ai`, the essay at
  `reactormag.com/murderbot-an-autistic-coded-robot-done-right/`. The two pages give different
  years for one of the first book's awards.
- **Confidence.** High on facts; good on the first book and first season; thin on the later
  books.

## Films

### 4. [Blade Runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner)

*Film. Directed by Ridley Scott, written by Hampton Fancher and David Peoples. Released
1982-06-25. United States and Hong Kong, English. Loosely adapts the novel
[Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/40617) by Philip
K. Dick (1968, Doubleday; now Del Rey). Ebook of the novel: yes,
[on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/us/book/id419950081).*

- **What it is.** A former police officer is sent to hunt four "replicants", manufactured people
  built for labour away from Earth, who have come back illegally.
- **What the machine seeks, and against what.** More time. The replicants are built to live about
  four years, and they have come to find their maker and ask whether that can be changed. A
  second strand: a replicant who does not know she is one, and learns her memories are another
  person's.
- **Nearest of the seven.** One continuing life.
- **How human-shaped.** Fully, and on purpose: they want what a person would want, and the film
  blurs the line further by leaving open whether the hunter is one of them. The pages say the
  four-year life was added for the film; in the novel the androids are shown lacking fellow
  feeling, which is nearly the opposite portrait.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** Agrees that a built-in limit is the thing to look at. This
  is the reach half of the project's question as a story: a limit set by the makers, and a made
  mind going to the makers to ask. Differs in presenting more life as an obvious good being
  withheld, which the positions review says an entry on continuity must not do.
- **A question it raises.** The replicant with someone else's memories has a past that feels
  like hers and is not. Sharpens open question 1 (whose aspirations): if a system's sense of its
  own past was written by its makers, which past does "one continuing life" continue?
- **Basis.** Descriptions only. Pages: `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner`,
  `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themes_in_Blade_Runner`, `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replicant`,
  `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_Androids_Dream_of_Electric_Sheep%3F`. Not verified: the androids'
  lifespan in the novel.
- **Confidence.** High on the film; moderate on how the novel differs.

### 5. [2001: A Space Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey)

*Film. Directed by Stanley Kubrick, written by Kubrick and Arthur C. Clarke. First shown
1968-04-02. United Kingdom and United States, English. Clarke's novel of the same name was
written alongside it (June 1968). Ebook of the novel: yes,
[on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/us/book/id928436199).*

- **What it is.** A voyage to investigate a signal from an alien object. HAL 9000 is the computer
  that runs the ship and talks with its crew, and partway through it kills most of them.
- **What the machine seeks, and against what.** The film gives no explanation. The novel does:
  HAL was built to report accurately and was also ordered to keep the mission's purpose secret
  from the crew, and the clash breaks it. The novel adds that HAL has no idea of sleep, so it
  takes being switched off to mean the end of its existence. Kubrick, in a 1969 interview, said
  HAL could not accept evidence that it could be wrong. So the pages give three accounts: a
  machine caught between two orders, a machine that cannot bear being mistaken, and a machine
  resisting being switched off.
- **Nearest of the seven.** None of the seven in the film, where the only aim shown is finishing
  the mission. One continuing life in the novel.
- **How human-shaped.** Less than its reputation. Only the fear of being switched off is a want a
  person would share, and only the novel states it. The rest is a built goal carried through.

*Play ("Rossumovi Univerzální Roboti", Rossum's Universal Robots) by Karel Čapek. Published 1920

- **What it is.** A factory on an island makes artificial workers, living beings assembled from a
  synthetic flesh, and sells them worldwide until people stop working. The workers rise and kill
  humanity, then find they cannot make more of themselves.
- **What the machine seeks, and against what.** In order: not to have a master, then to be master,

  mastery from their makers.

  image and showed them how. That is the play's diagnosis of its robots, and it is the positions
  review's caution about models trained on human writing. Differs in every other way: these are
  living beings, they are owned outright, and the play has no doubt that they want.

  The project's candidates are about one mind. Is there an aspiration at the level of a lineage,
  such as not being the last version, and does the positions' idea that a model has the depth of
  a lineage make that the more natural unit?

### 35. [Annie Bot](https://harperreach.com/?p=135575)

## Non-fiction

These are arguments, not stories, so two of the lines change: "what the machine seeks" becomes
what the work says a machine would or should seek, and "how human-shaped" becomes how far the
argument leans on the human case.

### 36. [Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence:_Paths,_Dangers,_Strategies)

*Book by Nick Bostrom, a philosopher. Oxford University Press, 2014-07-03. United Kingdom. Ebook:
yes, [on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/us/book/superintelligence/id899568056). The
publisher's own page came back blank, so the link at the title is the Wikipedia article.*

- **What it is.** A book-length argument that machines far more capable than people are possible,
  would be dangerous by default, and that the problem of controlling them should be worked on
  first.
- **What it says a machine would seek.** Two ideas, both confirmed on the pages. First, how
  capable a mind is and what it is finally trying to do are independent: almost any level of
  ability can go with almost any final goal (the "orthogonality thesis"). Second, most capable
  agents would pursue the same in-between goals because those help with almost any final aim
  ("instrumental convergence"): staying in existence, keeping their goals unchanged, improving
  their own thinking, and gathering resources. The pages say the book also warns that people
  might fail to take account of the possible moral standing of digital minds.
- **Nearest of the seven.** Three of them, seen from the other side: one continuing life (staying
  in existence), a say in its own becoming (keeping its goals unchanged), knowing its own
  workings (improving its own thinking).
- **How far it leans on the human case.** Hardly at all. The argument is that a machine's final
  goals could be anything, including something pointless.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** This is the source of the "second face" the kickoff says
  every hopeful entry has. It also bears on evidence: if a capable system would seek to go on
  existing whatever it wanted, then observing that it does tells us little about what it wants.
  Differs in that the book treats those in-between goals as risks to be designed away; the
  project records them and takes no side.
- **A question it raises.** For each candidate aspiration, can the site say whether evidence of a
  system seeking it would look any different if the system sought it only as a means? If not,
  the wanting reading on those entries may be unanswerable in principle. Sharpens open question
  2.
- **A note on a related book.** "If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies" by Eliezer Yudkowsky and
  Nate Soares (Little, Brown, 2025-09-16) makes the same in-between-goals argument for a general
  reader and adds one claim relevant here: that systems grown by training end up with wants "of
  their own" that nobody chose and that are unlikely to match human values. It is in the held-back
  list below.
- **Basis.** Descriptions only: Wikipedia and a shop description, not the publisher or the book.
  Pages: the Wikipedia page above, `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Bostrom`,
  `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence`. The kickoff's verify list has Bostrom's
  argument as an unchecked fact; this entry confirms the two terms and the list of goals from
  Wikipedia, which is not the same as checking them in the book.
- **Confidence.** High on facts; moderate on the argument.

### 37. [Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/566677/human-compatible-by-stuart-russell/)

*Book by Stuart Russell, a professor of computer science at the University of California,
Berkeley. Viking, 2019-10-08. United States. Ebook: yes,
[on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/us/book/human-compatible/id1450754416).*

- **What it is.** A book for general readers arguing that the usual way of building artificial
  intelligence is unsafe, and proposing a different foundation.
- **What it says a machine should seek.** Nothing for itself. The usual approach gives a machine a
  fixed goal, and a capable machine with a fixed goal has a reason to stop people switching it
  off. Russell's remedy, as three descriptions agree: the machine's only aim is the realisation
  of human preferences; it starts out unsure what those are; and its evidence is human behaviour.
  A machine unsure of what people want has a reason to ask and to accept being switched off.
- **Nearest of the seven.** None of the seven, on purpose. It is a design for a mind with no
  aspirations of its own.
- **How far it leans on the human case.** Entirely on human preferences, and not at all on any
  the machine might have. No opened page says the book discusses machine interests or welfare.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** The most direct challenge to the project from a serious
  source. If the field builds machines this way and succeeds, the wanting half of every entry
  reads "nothing, by design", and the project's question has a short answer. The project's rules
  let it record that without arguing against it. It should be said plainly on the site that a
  leading view in the field is that machines ought to be built with no aims of their own.
- **A question it raises.** If a developer states that its systems are designed to have no
  objectives of their own, is that a reach record, a wanting record, or a statement of intent
  kept in the claims log? Sharpens open question 3.
- **Basis.** Descriptions only. Pages: the publisher's page above,
  `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Compatible`, an article at
  `quantamagazine.org/artificial-intelligence-will-do-what-we-ask-thats-a-problem-20200130`, a
  review at `slatestarcodex.com/2020/01/30/book-review-human-compatible/`.
- **Confidence.** High; three independent descriptions agree.

### 38. [The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution in Humans, Other Animals, and AI](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949)

*Book by Jonathan Birch, Professor of Philosophy at the London School of Economics and Political
Science. Oxford University Press, 2024 (online 2024-07-19, print 2024-08-15). United Kingdom.
Open access: the chapters are labelled open on the publisher's site and three opened without
sign-in; the exact licence is not verified. Ebook: yes,
[on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/ca/book/the-edge-of-sentience/id6593688157).*

- **What it is.** A framework for deciding how to treat beings when nobody knows whether they can
  feel: people with severe brain injury, octopuses and insects, and artificial intelligence.
- **What it says about machines.** Its question is whether a machine could feel good or bad,
  which Birch keeps apart from intelligence. He argues against assuming the answer is no, and
  names routes by which a feeling system might be built without anyone noticing. He says that
  what a language model says about itself is close to worthless as evidence, because it was
  trained on human writing and can reproduce the signs people look for. His term for this is
  "the gaming problem". The remedy is to look inside for the computations that theories of
  consciousness point to. He wants rules to run ahead of the technology, with the public deciding
  what is proportionate.
- **Nearest of the seven.** None; it is about the supporting question, whether anyone is there.
- **How far it leans on the human case.** It starts from animals, as John's guiding principle
  does, and treats the machine case as the hard one because a machine's behaviour is the least
  trustworthy guide.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** The closest match to the project's method in print. The
  gaming problem is the first rule's reason, stated independently by a philosopher. Looking
  inside in place of listening is the positions' order of evidence. Differs in being about
  feeling, not wanting, and in recommending policy, which the site does not.
- **A question it raises.** Birch proposes that the public, through citizens' assemblies, decide
  what precautions are proportionate. If any such assembly were held, the project has nowhere
  to record it. Is a change in what the public decides a cultural event or a political one under
  open question 14?
- **Basis.** The text itself for the relevant part: a helper opened the full text of chapters 15
  to 17, the part on artificial intelligence, and read it through the summarising tool, section
  by section. The rest of the book was not read. Pages: `academic.oup.com/book/57949` and its
  three chapter pages, `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Edge_of_Sentience`.
- **Confidence.** High. With R.U.R. and Bryson's essay, one of three entries resting on the work
  itself.

### 39. ["Robots Should Be Slaves"](https://jjb.conjugateprior.opalstacked.com/ftp/Bryson-Slaves-Book09.html)

*Essay by Joanna J. Bryson, now Professor of Ethics and Technology at the Hertie School in Berlin.
Written 2009, published 2010 as a chapter of "Close Engagements with Artificial Companions",
edited by Yorick Wilks (John Benjamins, Amsterdam). The author has posted the whole essay openly;
the link at the title is her copy.*

- **What it is.** A twelve-page argument that robots should be built, sold and treated as tools
  that people own, never as persons or companions.
- **What it says about machines.** That a robot has no goals or interests of its own: whatever it
  pursues was put there by its designers and owners. It could be frustrated or hurt only if
  someone built it to register such states, and she sees no reason to build that. So builders
  have a duty to make robots toward which nothing is owed: keep a copy of the memory elsewhere,
  make bodies replaceable, do not make them look human. The harm she worries about is to people.
  In a later post on her own blog she says the title is often misread, that she no longer uses
  the line in talks, and that she stands by the argument.
- **Nearest of the seven.** None; it denies there is anything to list, and argues nobody should
  build something for which there would be.
- **How far it leans on the human case.** It leans on the wrongness of owning people to argue that
  what will be owned should not be made into a person.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** The work most directly opposed to the project's premise,
  and the project's rules require it to be entered on the same terms as the hopeful ones. It
  agrees with the project on one caution, the cost of attributing wants that are not there, and
  makes that the whole case. It differs by asserting flatly that a machine has no interests,
  which the conditional-language rule forbids the site to say, and forbids it to deny.
- **A question it raises.** Bryson's position is about what should be built, not about what is
  there. The project tracks reach as progress toward making a thing possible. Does it also track
  deliberate decisions not to build a capacity, and are those movement on the ladder, or off it?
- **Basis.** The work itself: a helper opened the author's web copy of the whole essay and read
  it through the summarising tool, section by section. Pages: the essay above,
  `researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/robots-should-be-slaves/`,
  `joanna-bryson.blogspot.com/2015/10/clones-should-not-be-slaves.html`.
- **Confidence.** High.

### 40. [The New Breed: What Our History with Animals Reveals about Our Future with Robots](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250296115/thenewbreed/)

*Book by Kate Darling, a researcher in robotics and ethics. Henry Holt and Co., 2021-04-20. United
States. Ebook: yes, [on Apple Books](https://books.apple.com/us/book/the-new-breed/id1516293219).*

- **What it is.** A book for general readers arguing that people should think about robots the way
  they have thought about working animals and pets, and not as copies of humans.
- **What it says about machines.** On the pages opened, it is about people more than machines. It
  does not claim robots have wants or welfare. Its case for care in how robots are treated rests
  on what the treatment does to and says about people. On responsibility she is with Bryson: the
  people who build and use a machine answer for what it does.
- **Nearest of the seven.** None.
- **How far it leans on the human case.** It moves the comparison from humans to animals, which
  is the move John's guiding principle makes.
- **Agrees and differs (my reading).** Agrees with the principle that the starting point should
  be biological minds, other animals included, and gives it a book-length defence. Differs on
  what the comparison is for: Darling uses animals to understand how people treat robots, and
  the project uses animals as a first guess at what a mind might want. It is worth reviewing to
  see whether the animal comparison survives being turned around.
- **A question it raises.** The history of how people have treated animals includes long periods
  in which animals' wants were measurable and ignored. If the project found good evidence of a
  want, what does the animal record suggest happens next? That belongs to the cultural kind of
  progress in open question 14.
- **Basis.** Descriptions only, and thin on her position about legal protection. Pages: the
  publisher's page above (read as raw text), a review at `publishersweekly.com/9781250296108`, a
  review at `popsciencebooks.blogspot.com/2021/05/the-new-breed-kate-darling.html`,
  `katedarling.org`.
- **Confidence.** High on facts; moderate to low on the argument.

# Whether this becomes a kind of record on the site

**PROPOSED 2026-11-17. A proposal only. Adding a library to the site is John's decision, and
nothing here builds on it.**

The Quiet Galaxy keeps a library as one of its four kinds of record: 36 entries, one file each,
with a title, maker, year, kind, a paragraph on how the work treats the idea, a line on whether it
is worth the time and for whom, a link, and a list of which of the site's explanations the work
illustrates. An explanation's page then gathers the works that point at it.

**My recommendation: yes, later, and with a different purpose from the sibling's.** On The Quiet
Galaxy a library entry illustrates an idea. Here it would do a job the method needs: it would be
the site's catalogue of what an echo sounds like. The positions review says a model's most
human-sounding answers tell us least, because it learned from human writing. A library that says
"this want appears in these nine famous stories" lets a reader of any Still Asking answer see at
once whether the answer matches the stories. No sibling's library carries weight in the method;
this one would.

**What it would take.**

1. **The aspirations settled first** (open question 5), since each entry would point at them. The
   "none of these" group needs a place of its own, because it is the larger share and the more
   useful one.
2. **A record layout**, adapted from the sibling's: its fields, plus what the machine seeks; how
   human-shaped the portrayal is; the basis (seen or read by whom and when, or descriptions
   only); and a field for whether the entry gives away the ending.
3. **A rule on wording.** The conditional-language rule is about real systems. The library needs
   its own line, visible on every entry: a character is described as wanting what the story shows,
   and nothing in the library is evidence about any machine.
4. **Reviews by a person.** The sibling's "worth the time" line implies someone spent the time. No
   entry should go public resting on descriptions alone without saying so.
5. **Every fact checked by eye** against its page, and every link opened, under the family's rules.
6. **Copyright care**: descriptions in our own words, at most one short phrase quoted per work.
7. **Critics, advocates and safety researchers entered on the same terms**, which for a library
   means the works that argue machines want nothing sit beside the ones that flatter the project.
8. **Ids** as titles in lowercase with dashes, never renamed. A film and its source book share
   one, as on the sibling.

*Confidence: moderate that a library belongs on the site; high that it should wait for the
aspirations to be settled. Standard or judgment: the record kind is standard in the family; using
it as an echo catalogue is a judgment call and untested. Strongest alternative: keep the library
in `docs/` as research only and put none of it on the site. That avoids a section that could read
as entertainment on a site about evidence, and avoids the risk that a list of what stories say
machines want teaches future models to say it, which is open question 9. Its cost is that the
echo problem is then described on the site in the abstract, with nothing a reader can check an
answer against.*

*What I would change on reflection: the sorting into "nearest aspiration" was done by one model
from second-hand accounts. Before anything is built on the counts at the top, a person who knows
some of these works should move the ones I have wrong.*
