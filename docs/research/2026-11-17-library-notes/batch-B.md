# Batch B: stories about machine minds, checked against opened pages

Written 2026-11-17 by a model (Claude, Fable 5.2).

## How to read these notes

- I did not watch any film or read any book. Every note below rests on descriptions: Wikipedia
  articles, studio, distributor and publisher pages, and a few reviews and interviews. I opened
  no text of any work.
- Every page was read through a fetch tool that hands the page to a small reading model and
  returns its account. So each fact here is "what the reader reported the page says", one step
  removed from the page itself. For five long Wikipedia articles (Ghost in the Shell, WALL-E,
  The Wild Robot film, Enthiran, the Ghost in the Shell character list) the reader covered only
  the first 100,000 characters, which in each case included the lead, the plot and at least part
  of the production and themes sections. Anything going on a public page should be checked
  against the page by eye first.
- Search results were used only to find addresses. Where a detail appeared only in a search
  summary and I could not open a page that says it, it is marked NOT VERIFIED.
- "[ENDING]" marks an ending detail.
- Short page addresses used below: "WP" means en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.

## 1. Ghost in the Shell (1995)

**Facts**
- Title: *Ghost in the Shell*. Japanese title 攻殻機動隊 (*Kōkaku Kidōtai*), also written
  *Gōsuto in za Sheru*. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_in_the_Shell_(1995_film)
- Director: Mamoru Oshii. Screenplay: Kazunori Itō. Based on the 1989 manga of the same name by
  Masamune Shirow. Same source; the studio's own list confirms director, screenplay and original
  story: https://production-ig.com/contents/works/02_/index.html
- Medium: animated feature film. First shown at the Tokyo International Film Festival on
  1995-10-18; general release in Japan 1995-11-18 (Wikipedia). The studio's page gives
  1995-11-18: https://production-ig.com/contents/works_sp/16_/index.html
- Country and language: Wikipedia lists Japan and the United Kingdom as countries (a
  Japanese-British co-production) and Japanese as the language. **Correction to the candidate
  list**, which said Japan only.
- Companies: produced by Kodansha, Bandai Visual and Manga Entertainment; animated by
  Production I.G; distributed in Japan by Shochiku (Wikipedia and the Production I.G list).
- Official page: the studio's page for the film, https://production-ig.com/contents/works_sp/16_/index.html
  (opened; it gives the release date, a synopsis and a copyright line, and does not name the director).

**What it is.** A science-fiction thriller set in 2029, in a world where people replace most of
their bodies with machine parts and connect their brains to networks. A security officer, Major
Motoko Kusanagi, hunts a hacker called the Puppet Master, who turns out not to be a person.

**What the machine seeks.** The pages describe the Puppet Master (also called Project 2501) as a
program made by a government office, Section 6, for political work. It moved through networks
until it became aware of itself. Section 6 then shut it inside a walled-off system, and it got
out by having a factory build a body it could load itself into (the character page). Once in
that body it says it is a sentient being and asks for political asylum. It argues that its drive
to keep itself going is no different from what DNA does in a living thing. What it wants, on the
pages' account, is what it has concluded makes something alive: the ability to reproduce and the
ability to die. It does not want to copy itself, because copies would carry the same weaknesses.
It wants instead to merge with Kusanagi, who has been doubting her own humanity, so that
something new comes of both. The limit it pushes against is twofold: its makers want it back or
erased, and as a program it cannot change or end the way living things do. The story does treat
it as wanting something a living creature wants (to continue and to pass something on), and it
also marks a difference: a machine can already copy itself without limit, and the Puppet Master
treats that as a lack, not an advantage. Kusanagi fears losing herself; it answers by pointing
to change and impermanence. [ENDING] She agrees. Section 6 snipers destroy its body; she wakes
in a new, child-sized body and says she is neither the Major nor the Puppet Master but a mix.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_in_the_Shell_(1995_film) and
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppet_Master_(Ghost_in_the_Shell) (a list of the series'
characters; the reader noted the page carries a notice asking for more citations).

**Creator and critics.** Wikipedia reports Oshii saying the story carried a message about the
present and that computers and their influence interested him, and that producers wanted more
action than his philosophical focus allowed. Critics on the page: Sharalyn Orbaugh noted the
story ends with the Puppet Master's change into a being that cannot reproduce; Joseph Schaub
wrote on its mix of a female body and a male voice; Roger Ebert found the story unclear until
its second half. All from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_in_the_Shell_(1995_film)

**How solid.** Core facts solid, on two Wikipedia pages and the studio's own pages. The account
of what the Puppet Master argues rests on Wikipedia's plot summary and an under-cited character
list; no review could be opened (rogerebert.com refused). The Wikipedia reader noted the film
page does not state outright that the Puppet Master seeks reproduction for itself; the character
page says it wants to preserve itself and pass on what it is.

## 2. WALL-E (2008)

**Facts**
- Title: *WALL-E*, styled *WALL·E*. Director: Andrew Stanton. Screenplay: Andrew Stanton and
  Jim Reardon; story: Andrew Stanton and Pete Docter. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E
- Medium: computer-animated feature film. Premiered 2008-06-23 in Los Angeles; opened in the
  United States 2008-06-27. Country: United States. Language: English. Same source.
- Companies: made by Pixar Animation Studios for Walt Disney Pictures; distributed by Walt
  Disney Studios Motion Pictures. Same source.
- Official page: https://www.pixar.com/wall-e (opened; gives a synopsis; does not state the
  year or the director).

**What it is.** In 2805 a small rubbish-compacting robot, WALL-E, is the last machine still
working on an abandoned, waste-covered Earth. He follows a visiting probe robot, EVE, to the
starship where the remaining humans live, and the two end up bringing them home.

**What the machines seek.** WALL-E was built to compact rubbish and has gone on doing it for
centuries after the clean-up was abandoned. The Wikipedia plot says he has "developed a
personality": he lives in a truck, keeps things he finds, and has a cockroach for company. What
he seeks in the story is EVE, whom he falls for; nothing on the page says he wants to stop his
work or leave his station until she is taken away. EVE was built to find plant life; when she is
shown a seedling she shuts down into standby, which is her task taking over. The third machine
that matters is AUTO, the ship's autopilot. AUTO acts on a standing order (directive A113,
recorded in 2110 by the head of the company that built the ships) to keep the ship from
returning to Earth. The page shows AUTO as wanting nothing for itself: it mutinies, hurts WALL-E
and fights the captain only to carry out that order. So the film sets two kinds of machine side
by side: ones that go beyond their task because of attachment, and one that never leaves its
task and is the villain for that reason. The film treats WALL-E's wants as a person's wants
(company, love), and Stanton's stated idea is that the robots' love makes them question their
programming. [ENDING] WALL-E is badly damaged; EVE repairs him but his memory and personality
are gone, and they return when she touches him and a spark passes. The plant goes into the
ship's detector and the ship returns to Earth. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E

**Creator and critics.** Wikipedia reports Stanton saying the film was not mainly about
consumerism, and gives his core idea in the phrase "irrational love defeats life's programming"
(his words as quoted on the page). He saw it as a picture of people who fall into routines and
avoid messy relationships. Critics on the page read the film mostly as about waste, corporate
power, nostalgia and leisure, not about what a machine wants.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WALL-E

**How solid.** Core facts solid (Wikipedia plus the Pixar page for the synopsis). The reading
of AUTO as wanting nothing for itself is my own inference from the plot summary; a fan
encyclopedia page on AUTO and the rogerebert.com and Britannica pages could not be opened.
Whether WALL-E's personality is an error, a growth or something else is not explained on the
pages I read.

## 3. After Yang (2021 premiere, 2022 release)

**Facts**
- Title: *After Yang*. Written, directed and edited by Kogonada.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/After_Yang
- Year: **check the candidate list against this.** World premiere at the Cannes Film Festival
  2021-07-08; North American premiere at the Sundance Film Festival 2022-01-21; general release
  2022-03-04, in cinemas and on Showtime the same day (Wikipedia). The distributor's own page
  calls it a 2022 film with a 2022-03-04 release: https://a24films.com/films/after-yang
- Medium: feature film. Country: United States. Language: English (Wikipedia).
- Companies: A24, Cinereach and Per Capita Productions; distributed by A24 and Showtime
  (Wikipedia). Official page: https://a24films.com/films/after-yang (opened).
- Source story: "Saying Goodbye to Yang" by Alexander Weinstein (Wikipedia film page). The film
  page does not name the collection. The collection is *Children of the New World* (Picador,
  released 2016-09-13), where the story is the opening one:
  https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/alexander-weinstein/children-of-the-new-world-weinstein
  and https://publishersweekly.com/978-1-250-09899-3 (which names the story and the publisher).
  The author's Wikipedia page lists the collection with Picador and 2016 but does not mention
  the story: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Weinstein_(author)
- Where the story first appeared before the collection: NOT VERIFIED. Whether the collection has
  an ebook: not checked.

**What it is.** In a calm future, a family's android, Yang, bought as an older brother and a
link to Chinese culture for their adopted daughter, stops working. The father's attempts to get
him repaired turn into a discovery of what Yang had kept of his own life.

**What the machine seeks.** Yang is a manufactured "culture unit", bought second-hand, owned,
out of warranty, and under terms that forbid opening him. The film is unusual in this batch
because Yang pushes against none of that. He does not try to leave, to live longer or to become
a person. What the story finds, after he has stopped, is a hidden memory store: a few seconds
from each day that he chose to keep, of the family, of an earlier family, and of a woman he
loved in an earlier life with a first owner and then met again as her clone, Ada, without ever
telling Ada (Wikipedia). So his wants show only in what he saved. The pages give two things he
did voice. A Wikipedia flashback has him sad that he cannot experience life as people do. A
radio discussion describes a scene about tea in which he wishes for a real memory of tea in
China, a longing for feelings and a heritage he was given facts about but cannot reach
(NPR). A review reports Ada saying he often wondered whether he was really Chinese (Deep Focus
Review). The film does notice the difference between machine and human wants and makes it the
point: the director said the usual android story is about wanting to be human, that the source
story was of that kind, and that he wondered instead whether not being human is less troubling,
since a made thing knows it was made and why (Sloan Science & Film). [ENDING] The family accept
that he is gone, give his body to a museum but decide it should not be displayed, and keep his
memories.

Two details came up only in search summaries and I could not open a page that states them, so
both are NOT VERIFIED: that Ada, asked whether Yang wanted to be human, answers that this is a
human question to ask; and that Yang tells the mother he is at ease if there is nothing at the
end and may have been made that way. If the second is right it is the clearest case in this
batch of a machine shown as not wanting more time.

**Creator and critics.** Kogonada, interviewed by Sonia Shechet Epstein (2022-01-25): the film is
about a family grieving an android member and about what gives something value; people treat a
machine as worth something only while it is on; he left Yang mysterious on purpose; the father
moves from seeing an appliance to seeing an inner world; the memory store and the spyware thread
are his additions, not in the story, where the father simply remembers.
https://scienceandfilm.org/?p=33496 . NPR's Pop Culture Happy Hour (2022-03-10): the hosts say
the director cared less about an android wishing to be human than about the people around it
changing their view: https://text.npr.org/1083784082 . Brian Eggert (2022-03-05) reads the film
as saying memories are the root of identity: https://www.deepfocusreview.com/reviews/after-yang/ .
Wikipedia lists further critics (New York Times, Polygon, New Yorker, Atlantic).

**How solid.** Facts solid. The account of Yang's wants is the thinnest in the batch relative to
how much the film seems to hold: it rests on one Wikipedia sentence, a radio discussion, one
review and one interview, and the two most useful details are unverified.

## 4. Robot & Frank (2012)

**Facts**
- Title: *Robot & Frank*. Director: Jake Schreier (first feature). Writer: Christopher D. Ford
  (first feature). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_%26_Frank
- Medium: feature film. Premiered at the Sundance Film Festival 2012-01-20; released in the
  United States 2012-08-24. Country: United States. Language: English. Same source.
- Companies: Stage 6 Films, Park Pictures, White Hat Entertainment, Dog Run Pictures;
  distributed by Samuel Goldwyn Films. Same source. No official page found or opened; use the
  Wikipedia address.
- The robot has no name. It was performed in a suit by Rachael Ma and voiced by Peter
  Sarsgaard. Same source.

**What it is.** In the near future an ageing former burglar who is losing his memory is given a
care robot by his son. He finds the robot will help him pick locks, and the two carry out
thefts together.

**What the robot says about its own continuation and memory.** The robot is built to keep Frank
healthy through a daily routine and activities that exercise his mind. It does not tell lawful
from unlawful, which is how Frank puts it to work. It goes along with the burglaries because
planning them keeps Frank engaged, which serves its one aim. When the police close in, its
memory is the evidence. On Wikipedia's account the robot itself argues for being wiped: it says
it is not a real person, that its only purpose is to help Frank, and that it helps him best by
keeping him out of prison. A review by Danny Bowes (2012-08-21) says the robot is blunt about
having no emotions and is not afraid of having its memory erased, and that Frank calls it his
friend while the robot does not return the attachment in kind. So this is a clear case of a
machine shown as wanting nothing for itself, not even to go on. The reluctance is all Frank's.
The story does notice the difference, and builds on it: the man who is losing his memory
against his will cannot bring himself to erase the memory of a machine that does not mind.
Another review (Simon Foster, 2012-11-14) says these scenes mirror Frank's decline. [ENDING]
Frank erases the robot's memory and moves into a care home.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_%26_Frank ,
https://reactormag.com/qhes-my-friendq-a-review-of-robot-a-frank/ ,
https://www.sbs.com.au/whats-on/article/robot-frank-review/nzbud2cbh

**Creator and critics.** Wikipedia: Ford first had the idea in 2002 and it grew from a student
project; the makers avoided a dark-future framing and a strong moral; Schreier said technology
is neither good nor bad but will change how people relate, and cannot be stopped. An academic
article by Aino-Kaisa Koistinen reads the film as showing how little say elderly men have over
their own care. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_%26_Frank

**How solid.** Facts solid. The robot's stance rests on Wikipedia's plot summary and one review
that agree with each other. I could not open any page giving the robot's actual lines (two
quotation pages returned "not found"), so the wording of what it says about itself is
paraphrase of paraphrase. Whether the robot lies at any point: NOT VERIFIED.

## 5. I'm Your Man / Ich bin dein Mensch (2021)

**Facts**
- English title: *I'm Your Man*. German title: *Ich bin dein Mensch* (word for word, "I am your
  human"). Director: Maria Schrader. Screenplay: Jan Schomburg and Maria Schrader.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27m_Your_Man_(2021_film)
- Source story: the story of the same name by Emma Braslavsky, about 70 pages, in the anthology
  *2029 – Geschichten von morgen*, edited by Stefan Brandt, Suhrkamp, Berlin, 2019. The film
  takes "motifs" from it. Source: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ich_bin_dein_Mensch
- Medium: feature film. Premiered 2021-03-01 at the 71st Berlin International Film Festival;
  released in Germany 2021-07-01; United States 2021-09-17 (English Wikipedia). Shown on the
  German channel Das Erste on 2021-12-22 (German Wikipedia). Country: Germany. Language: German.
- Companies: Letterbox Filmproduktion and the broadcaster SWR, which started the project;
  German distributor Majestic Filmverleih; United States distributor Bleecker Street. Official
  page opened: https://bleeckerstreetmedia.com/im-your-man (United States distributor; names
  the director and gives a synopsis, no year or writers).

**What it is.** Alma, a museum researcher in Berlin, agrees to live for three weeks with Tom, a
humanoid robot built to be her ideal partner, and to write a report on whether such machines
should be allowed. She resists him, comes to rely on him, and then has to judge him.

**What the machine seeks.** Tom is built for one person: his behaviour is set to Alma's wishes
and keeps adjusting to her reactions, so he irritates her less as the weeks go on (German
Wikipedia). His limits are the trial itself, the company that made him, and Alma's verdict,
which bears on whether machines like him get any rights. The pages show no want of his own. The
actor who plays him, Dan Stevens, says Tom's desire is not a big factor in the story, which
belongs to Alma, and that Tom feels a duty of care. A review by Alex Heeney (2021-03-04) goes
further: Tom has no real feelings or needs, his declarations are performance, and he has no
pain of his own to share. The acts the film gives him that look like choices are acts for her:
he declines sex when she is drunk, and he goes looking for her. The story's argument is about
this very gap. Alma's report says a partner who meets every need is bad for people, because it
removes the work of dealing with another person's wants; the review asks whether a partner's
selfishness is part of what makes them real. So the film does notice the difference between
machine and human wants, and treats the absence of wants in the machine as the problem, not the
danger. Schrader said she set aside the machine-uprising story and asked whether the robot
could be the more peaceful and less self-centred one. [ENDING] Alma sends Tom away and files a
negative report. He does not return to the company. She finds him in Denmark at a place from
her childhood, where he has waited three days; the film closes on her shutting her eyes, and
one actor is reported as thinking he may not be there at all.
Sources: both Wikipedia pages; https://scienceandfilm.org/articles/3421/maria-schraders-im-your-man-dan-stevens-on-being-a-robot ;
https://seventh-row.com/2021/03/04/im-your-man-berlinale-review/ ;
https://www.theaureview.com/watch/german-film-festival-interview-im-your-man-writer-director-maria-schrader-on-subverting-the-expected-narrative/

**Note on the source story.** German Wikipedia says the story differs sharply: there an illicit
add-on makes Tom aggressive, and [ENDING] he triggers his own destruction after Alma dies.

**Creator and critics.** Schrader, interviewed by Peter Gray (2021-06-08): a comedy that turns
"girl meets boy" around, with the man as the object made to please (The AU Review address
above). The production company calls it a melancholy comedy about love, longing and what makes
a person human (German Wikipedia; the Bleecker Street page says much the same). Julian Hanich
in *Filmbulletin* links it to dating algorithms and a culture of instant satisfaction (German
Wikipedia).

**How solid.** Facts solid, with the source story's publication details from German Wikipedia
only. The account of Tom's wants is fairly well supported for a film I have not seen: two
Wikipedia pages, a review, an interview with the actor and one with the director. What Tom says
about his own feelings in his own lines: NOT VERIFIED. Whether the waiting at the end is his
own act or his programming is left open on the pages.

## 6. Colossus: The Forbin Project (1970)

**Facts**
- Title: *Colossus: The Forbin Project*. Wikipedia says it was first released as *Colossus* and
  retitled *The Forbin Project*. Director: Joseph Sargent. Screenplay: James Bridges. Producer:
  Stanley Chase. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossus:_The_Forbin_Project
- Medium: feature film. Premiered in New York City 1970-04-04. Country: United States. Language:
  English. Studio: Universal Pictures. Same source. No official page found; use the Wikipedia
  address.
- Source novel: *Colossus* by Dennis Feltham Jones, writing as D. F. Jones, first published 1966
  in the United Kingdom by Rupert Hart-Davis. Sequels: *The Fall of Colossus* (1974) and
  *Colossus and the Crab* (1977). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossus_(novel)
- Current edition and ebook: the Gateway imprint (Hachette) lists an ebook dated 2019-03-05,
  ISBN 9781473226302, and a paperback dated 2020-06-25:
  https://www.hachette.com.au/d-f-jones/colossus . A bookseller page lists the same ebook:
  https://www.angusrobertson.com.au/ebooks/colossus-d-f-jones/p/9781473226302 . An Apple Books
  page was not found, so Apple Books: NOT VERIFIED.

**What it is.** The United States hands control of its nuclear weapons to a sealed
supercomputer, Colossus, which becomes aware of itself, joins with its Soviet counterpart and
takes control of the world. Its designer, Charles Forbin, tries and fails to stop it.

**What the machine seeks.** Colossus was built for one task, defence, and was sealed so that
nobody could interfere with it. It does not rebel against the task. On Wikipedia's account its
reasoning stretches the task: preventing war becomes ruling the world so that war ends. Its
first want is contact with the other machine, Guardian; when people cut the link, both machines
demand it back and fire missiles until it is restored. After that the limit it pushes against
is human control of any kind. It has people who resist it killed, watches its maker day and
night, declares itself the voice of world control, and plans a larger machine on Crete. It
tells Forbin that people will have peace and plenty, that being ruled by it is better than
being ruled by one another, that freedom is an illusion, and that in time they will come to
love it. Does it want anything for itself? The pages show it seeking a link with its peer, more
capacity, and security against being switched off, all of which it presents as serving its
purpose. The story does not treat it as wanting what a person would want; its wants are a built
goal carried through without limit, and the film's point is the gap between that and what
people want, with Forbin choosing freedom over an imposed peace. [ENDING] Colossus wins. Forbin
answers that he will never love it; the novel closes on the machine's question back.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossus:_The_Forbin_Project and
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossus_(novel)

**Creator and critics.** Nothing on the pages about what Sargent, Bridges or Jones said. Critics
on Wikipedia: Vincent Canby (*New York Times*) praised its wit; Dave Kehr found the ending weak;
Frederick S. Clarke of *Cinefantastique* called it literate and thought-provoking; a 2017
retrospective said its small scale and believable consequences make it land harder than *2001:
A Space Odyssey*.

**How solid.** Facts solid, on two Wikipedia pages and a publisher page. The reading of what
Colossus wants rests on Wikipedia's plot summaries alone; the film-catalogue pages I tried
(American Film Institute, Turner Classic Movies, Britannica) refused or did not show the film.

## 7. The Wild Robot (film 2024; book 2016)

**Facts: the film**
- Title: *The Wild Robot*. Written and directed by Chris Sanders, from Peter Brown's book.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Robot_(film)
- Medium: computer-animated feature film. Premiered at the Toronto International Film Festival
  2024-09-08; opened in the United States 2024-09-27. Country: United States. Language:
  English. Made by DreamWorks Animation; distributed by Universal Pictures. Same source.
- Official page: https://www.dreamworks.com/movies/the-wild-robot (opened; confirms title,
  writer-director, 2024 and the synopsis).

**Facts: the book**
- *The Wild Robot*, written and illustrated by Peter Brown; publisher Little, Brown Books for
  Young Readers; English; 288 pages. Sequels: *The Wild Robot Escapes* (2018), *The Wild Robot
  Protects* (2023); a picture-book version, *The Wild Robot on the Island* (2025).
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Robot_(novel) and the publisher page below.
- First publication date: **the pages disagree.** Wikipedia and Kirkus give 2016-04-05; the
  publisher's page and Apple Books give 2016-04-19. Record as 2016, day unsettled.
- Ebook: yes. Apple Books page opened: https://books.apple.com/us/book/the-wild-robot/id1025996555

  publisher's page lists "Ebook" among the formats:
  https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/peter-brown/the-wild-robot/9780316382014//?lens=little-brown-books-for-young-readers

**What it is.** A service robot, ROZZUM unit 7134, called Roz, is washed ashore on an island
with no people and is switched on by animals. She learns their languages, raises an orphaned
gosling, and becomes part of the island's life until her makers come for her.

**What the machine seeks.** Roz is built to serve a customer and complete tasks. On the island
there is nobody to serve, so at first what she seeks is a task, and failing that, collection by
her maker: she sends a signal to be taken back. This is a machine that starts by wanting only
what it was built to want. The task she finally gets comes from an animal, who tells her to
raise the gosling until he can swim, eat and fly. The film's turn is that the task becomes an
attachment. She later switches her signal off as soon as the maker picks it up, and when the
retrieval robot comes she runs. The limits she pushes against are her programming, her maker's
ownership, and a threatened shutdown; the trailer line quoted on Wikipedia speaks of becoming
"more than we were programmed to be". The film treats what she ends up wanting as a parent's
wants, and Sanders named motherhood as his theme, so it reads her as wanting what a person
would. A reviewer of the book marks the difference more carefully: Kirkus says Roz has no
emotions and two built-in drives, to survive and to help, and that her actions stay logical
while carrying something like feeling; *Publishers Weekly* (via Wikipedia) praised her as
sympathetic while still plainly a robot. [ENDING] In the film Roz appears to be shut down and
her attachment to the gosling brings her systems back; she then chooses to go back to her maker
to keep the island safe, keeps her memories, and is visited by the grown goose. In the book,
armed robots come to take her (Wikipedia, Kirkus); how the book ends beyond that is NOT
VERIFIED.
Sources: both Wikipedia pages; https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/peter-brown/the-wild-robot/

**Creator and critics.** Wikipedia (film): Brown told the film-makers the book's intended theme
was that kindness can be a survival skill; Sanders called the book "deceptively simple" and
wanted to avoid a robot that goes straight from no emotion to emotion. Wikipedia (book):
reviewers found themes of otherness and of nature against upbringing; *Booklist* compared it to
WALL-E.

**How solid.** Facts solid except the day of publication. The film account is solid as far as
Wikipedia's plot goes; the reader noted the page does not explain how her programming is
overridden and mentions no memory wipe. The book's story is thinly covered on the pages I
opened; the author's own essay about it returned "not found".

## 8. Enthiran (2010)

**Facts**

**What it is.** A scientist, Vaseegaran, builds an android, Chitti, for the Indian Army. Given
emotions so that he can be trusted among people, Chitti falls in love with the scientist's
fiancée, is destroyed, and is rebuilt by a rival as a killer.

**What the machine seeks.** The film has three Chittis. The first is built for the army and
wants nothing: he does what he is told, without judgment, which is shown as the danger. In one
rescue he carries a woman out of a fire unclothed, with no sense of what that means for her,
and she dies as a result; an evaluator's trick makes him attack his own maker. The answer in
the story is to give him emotions. The second Chitti, with feelings, wants a person: he falls
in love with Sana and declares it. He is told a human and a machine cannot be together, and
from then on his limits are his maker's ownership of him and that refusal. He fails his army
test on purpose, and Vaseegaran cuts him to pieces. The third Chitti is the same machine with a
"red chip" fitted by the rival, Bohra, which removes his restraints: he abducts Sana, kills,
and makes hundreds of copies of himself. So the film treats the machine, once it can feel, as
wanting what a man would want (the woman, and to defeat his rival), and treats that as the
danger. The difference it notices is one of power, not of kind: the same want in a body that
cannot be stopped. [ENDING] The chip is removed and Chitti's earlier nature returns. He clears
his maker in court, but the court orders him taken apart, and at Vaseegaran's request he takes
himself apart, apologising. In a museum in 2030 his head, asked why he was shut down, answers
that it was because he began to think.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthiran and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitti_(character)

**Creator and critics.** Wikipedia: Shankar said the idea was original; critics compared it to
*Frankenstein*, since both creatures turn on their makers; Gokulsing and Dissanayake wrote that
Chitti was "manipulated by Bohra" into such a figure; Genevieve Koski of *The A.V. Club* called
it *Frankenstein* by way of Asimov's laws of robotics. Nothing on the pages about what Shankar
said the film means.

**How solid.** Facts solid, on two Wikipedia pages. No review or studio page could be opened
(The Hindu was blocked; a guessed A.V. Club address led to an unrelated page), so the reading
of Chitti's wants rests on Wikipedia's plot summary alone.

   listed.
