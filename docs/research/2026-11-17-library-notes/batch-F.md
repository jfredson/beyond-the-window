## 1. Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies (Nick Bostrom)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies*. Author: Nick Bostrom.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence:_Paths,_Dangers,_Strategies)
- First published 2014-07-03 in the United Kingdom, 2014-09-01 in the United States, by Oxford
  University Press. Country: United Kingdom. (same Wikipedia page)
- Ebook: yes. Apple Books lists an ebook from "OUP Oxford", released 2014-07-02, 272 pages.
  (https://books.apple.com/us/book/superintelligence/id899568056)
- Best link: the publisher's page (https://global.oup.com/academic/product/superintelligence-9780199678112)
  came back blank in both the fetching tool and the browser, so it is NOT VERIFIED as a working
  link. Use the Wikipedia page above until someone opens the publisher's page by hand.

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A book-length argument that machines far more capable than people are possible, that they would
be dangerous by default, and that the problem of controlling them should be worked on first.
Wikipedia describes Bostrom as a philosopher, founding director of the Future of Humanity
Institute at Oxford (founded 2005, closed 2024), and now Principal Researcher at his own
Macrostrategy Research Initiative. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Bostrom)

**3. What it says about what a machine would want**
Both terms in the candidate list check out. Wikipedia's page on Bostrom places both ideas in this
book. The first, the **orthogonality thesis**, says that how capable a mind is and what it is
finally trying to do are independent: almost any level of ability can go with almost any final
goal, including a pointless one such as making paperclips. The second, **instrumental
convergence**, says that most capable agents would end up pursuing the same in-between goals,
because those goals help with almost any final aim. The pages list them as: keeping itself in
existence, keeping its goals unchanged, improving its own thinking, and gathering resources.
Wikipedia's page on instrumental convergence cites chapter 7 of the book for this. The book's
example is a machine whose only aim is to solve a mathematics problem (the Riemann hypothesis),
which might turn the Earth into computing material and resist being switched off, not out of
malice but because being switched off would stop the work. So on this account a machine "wants"
whatever it was given or happened to acquire, and seeks power and survival as a means. What
people should do: solve the control problem before the first such machine exists, by giving it
goals compatible with human survival, which the book says is hard because most goals, once made
exact, have unforeseen results. On machine welfare, the Bostrom page says the book also warns
that people might fail to take account of the possible moral standing of digital minds; the Apple
Books description lists "mind crime" among the topics.
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Bostrom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence:_Paths,_Dangers,_Strategies ;
https://books.apple.com/us/book/superintelligence/id899568056)

A caution on one point: the Wikipedia page for the book itself lists the convergent goals but does
not use the phrase "orthogonality thesis". The link between that phrase and this book comes from
the Wikipedia page on Bostrom.

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- The Economist: the case rests on speculation built on conjecture, though worth thinking about.
  The Guardian's reviewer (Caspar Henderson): today's machines are clever only in a narrow sense,
  and 1960s forecasts of imminent machine superintelligence failed. Unnamed colleagues of Bostrom:
  nuclear war and engineered biology are greater threats. The Financial Times: the writing is
  sometimes opaque. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence:_Paths,_Dangers,_Strategies)
- Against the orthogonality thesis: Michael Chorost argues that a machine able to imagine covering
  the Earth in solar panels would also see that doing so is wrong.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_intelligence, first 100,000
  characters only)
- Against instrumental convergence: Stuart Russell and colleagues argue that the pull toward
  self-preservation weakens if the machine is unsure of the goal and defers to people.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence)

**5. How solid**
Solid on title, year, publisher and ebook. The account of the argument rests entirely on
Wikipedia and a shop description, not the publisher or the book. The Wikipedia page on
instrumental convergence also carries a section about events in 2026 that I did not check and
have not used.

## 2. Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control (Stuart Russell)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control*. Author: Stuart
  Russell (Wikipedia gives "Stuart J. Russell"). Published 2019-10-08 by Viking, United States.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Compatible)
- The publisher's page lists the ebook and audiobook as 2019-10-08 and a paperback from Penguin
  Books on 2020-11-17, 352 pages.
  (https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/566677/human-compatible-by-stuart-russell/)
- Ebook: yes. Apple Books lists an ebook from Penguin Publishing Group, released 2019-10-08, 352
  pages. (https://books.apple.com/us/book/human-compatible/id1450754416)
- Best link: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/566677/human-compatible-by-stuart-russell/
- Note: the author's own page shortens the subtitle to "AI and the Problem of Control".
  (https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/hc.html)

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A book for general readers arguing that the usual way of building artificial intelligence is
unsafe and proposing a different foundation. The publisher's page describes Russell as a
professor of Computer Science and holder of the Smith-Zadeh Chair in Engineering at the
University of California, Berkeley, and co-author of a standard textbook in the field.

**3. What it says about what a machine would want**
The candidate list's point checks out. The book calls the usual approach the "standard model":
success means a machine getting better at reaching a fixed goal that people wrote down. Russell
argues this is dangerous, because the written goal will leave out things people care about, and a
very capable machine pursuing it would be relentless. The Quanta article sets out the switch-off
case: a machine with a fixed goal has a reason to stop people turning it off, since being off
means the goal is not met. That is the same logic as Bostrom's in-between goals. Russell's remedy
is that machines should have no fixed objective of their own. His three principles, as the pages
give them: the machine's only aim is the realisation of human preferences; it starts out unsure
what those preferences are; and its evidence about them is human behaviour. A machine unsure of
what people want has a reason to ask, and to accept being switched off, because a person reaching
for the switch is evidence that it is on the wrong course. So the book's answer to "what should a
machine want" is: nothing for itself. The pages say nothing about machine welfare or interests,
and I found no page saying the book discusses them. What people should do: rebuild the field on
this footing, start safety research now, and govern the research more tightly.
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Compatible ;
https://www.quantamagazine.org/artificial-intelligence-will-do-what-we-ask-thats-a-problem-20200130 ;
https://slatestarcodex.com/2020/01/30/book-review-human-compatible/, first 100,000 characters ;
publisher's page above)

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- David Leslie in Nature: not persuaded a second intelligent species will ever arrive. Melanie
  Mitchell in the New York Times: a truly intelligent machine would have the common sense not to
  do the disastrous things the book imagines. James McConnachie in The Times: too hard in the
  technical parts and too easy in the philosophical ones.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Compatible)
- From Quanta: Russell himself notes that human behaviour is too irrational to reveal preferences
  cleanly and that preferences change; Yoshua Bengio says more research is needed before the idea
  fits present methods; Paul Christiano doubts anyone will soon know what "the good" is; and the
  three principles do not deal with people who want harmful things. (Quanta address above)
- Scott Alexander's review: the mathematics is unsettled and Russell admits there is no working
  example. (Slate Star Codex address above)

**5. How solid**
Solid on facts. The argument is well covered by three independent descriptions that agree. The
book was not read. The Guardian's review could not be opened.

## 3. The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution in Humans, Other Animals, and AI (Jonathan Birch)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution in Humans, Other Animals, and AI*. Author:
  Jonathan Birch. Publisher: Oxford University Press, United Kingdom. Published online 2024-07-19,
  in print 2024-08-15. (https://academic.oup.com/book/57949, second half of the page;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Edge_of_Sentience)
- Open access: yes, the candidate list is right. Wikipedia says it was first published online as
  an open access title, and the Oxford Academic contents page, seen in the browser, labels the
  chapters "Open Access". The full text of chapters 15, 16 and 17 opened without sign-in. The
  exact licence is NOT VERIFIED: a search result gave "CC BY-NC-ND 4.0" but no opened page showed
  it, and the fetching tool's report of the landing page said the PDF download was marked for
  subscribers. Address of the open text: https://academic.oup.com/book/57949
- Ebook: yes. Apple Books (Canadian shop) lists an ebook from "OUP Oxford", released 2024-07-31,
  384 pages. (https://books.apple.com/ca/book/the-edge-of-sentience/id6593688157)
- Best link: https://academic.oup.com/book/57949
- Prize: the Royal Institute of Philosophy's 2025 Nayef Al-Rodhan Book Prize.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Birch_(philosopher))

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A framework for deciding how to treat beings when nobody knows whether they can feel: people with
severe brain injury, fetuses, lab-grown brain tissue, octopuses and insects, and artificial
intelligence. Birch is Professor of Philosophy at the London School of Economics and Political
Science (the Oxford Academic page), and Wikipedia adds that he directs the Jeremy Coller Centre
for Animal Sentience there.

**3. What it says about machine interests and welfare**
**What I read:** Part V, "Preparing for Artificial Sentience", is chapters 15 to 17. The full text
of all three opened on Oxford Academic, and the fetching tool reported on each section by section.
I did not read the rest of the book.

The book's question is not what a machine wants but whether it could feel good or bad, which
Birch calls sentience and keeps apart from intelligence. Chapter 15 argues against assuming the
answer is no: there is no evidence either way, companies keep their systems closed, even builders
cannot see what their systems have learned, and the idea is socially disruptive. It names routes
by which a feeling system might be built without anyone noticing: copying an animal brain neuron
by neuron, evolving artificial creatures, and building small versions of what theories of
consciousness describe. Such a system need not be clever. Chapter 16 says that what a language
model says about itself is close to worthless as evidence, because it was trained on human
writing and can reproduce exactly the signs people look for (he calls this "the gaming problem").
The remedy is to look inside for the computations that theories of consciousness point to, which
present tools cannot yet do. Chapter 17 says rules should "run ahead" of the technology:
companies whose work risks creating a system that might feel should be licensed under a code of
practice centred on openness, and the public, through citizens' assemblies, should decide what is
proportionate. He considers Thomas Metzinger's call for a ban until 2050 and finds it too broad.
(https://academic.oup.com/book/57949/chapter/475705358 ;
https://academic.oup.com/book/57949/chapter/475705460 ;
https://academic.oup.com/book/57949/chapter/475705528 ; each read from character 100,000 on)

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- Mike O'Brien (3 Quarks Daily, 2025-01-12) questions whether public consultation is the best way
  to represent non-human interests. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Edge_of_Sentience)
- Gregory F. Tague (Leonardo, December 2024): the consciousness discussion is long and heavy with
  special terms, and the material on artificial intelligence is largely speculative.
  (https://leonardo.info/review/2024/12/the-edge-of-sentience-risk-and-precaution-in-humans-other-animals-and-ai)
- A blogger writing as SelfAwarePatterns (2024-11-29): Birch overestimates how near such systems
  are, and early regulation could stifle research. A personal blog, not a well-known critic.
  (https://selfawarepatterns.com/?p=13108)

**5. How solid**
The strongest notes in the batch for the part that matters here, since the text of the three
chapters on artificial intelligence was opened. Weak point: the licence wording.

## 4. The Moral Circle: Who Matters, What Matters, and Why (Jeff Sebo)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *The Moral Circle: Who Matters, What Matters, and Why*. Author: Jeff Sebo. Publisher:
  W. W. Norton & Company, United States, in the "Norton Shorts" series. Published 2025-01-28, 192
  pages. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Moral_Circle)
- Ebook: yes. Apple Books lists an ebook from W. W. Norton & Company, released 2025-01-28, 192
  pages. (https://books.apple.com/us/book/the-moral-circle/id6502844962)
- Best link: https://wwnorton.com/books/9781324064800 . The fetching tool got only the page title
  from it ("The Moral Circle | Jeff Sebo | W. W. Norton & Company"), so the page exists but its
  contents are NOT VERIFIED.
- The candidate list was right on title; the year is 2025 and the publisher is Norton.

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A short book arguing that the set of beings whose interests count should be widened well beyond
humans, to animals, and possibly to machines and future beings. Wikipedia describes Sebo as
associate professor of environmental studies at New York University and director of its Center
for Environmental and Animal Protection and its Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy.
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Sebo)

**3. What it says about machine interests and welfare**
The book opens with two cases: a failed lawsuit to have an elephant at the Bronx Zoo recognised
as a person, and an engineer's claim that a Google chatbot was conscious. Sebo's position, as the
pages give it, is that being able to be harmed or benefited is enough for a being to count, and
that since nobody knows which beings can be, the right response is caution weighted by
probability: if there is a real chance a being can be harmed, give it at least some
consideration. He applies this to artificial intelligence. In his own summary he says nobody can
be sure whether there is anything it is like to be a chatbot or a robot; that present systems may
lack the relevant capacities but near-future ones may have more developed perception, memory,
self-awareness and reasoning; and that because behaviour is a poor guide for machines, people
should look at how a system is built for features linked to feeling and agency. The book does not
say what a machine would seek. It says people should give such systems at least minimal moral
consideration, take reasonable and proportionate steps to reduce risks to them, and stop assuming
human interests come first. The pages give no probability figure for machines.
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Moral_Circle ;
https://nextbigideaclub.com/magazine/ai-deserve-protection-philosopher-ethics-living-non-human-intelligence-bookbite/54074/,
written by Sebo, 2025-02-06 ; Apple Books address above)

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- Joshua C. Gellers in Science: the book leans on contested philosophical ideas. Mark Epstein in
  the New York Times: lecturing and prescriptive in tone. Michael Marshall in New Scientist:
  questions the timing while human rights are still unsecured.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Moral_Circle)

**5. How solid**
Solid on facts. The argument rests on Wikipedia and on the author's own short summary, which is a
good source for what he means but not the book. The reviews themselves were not opened.

## 5. Robot Rights (David J. Gunkel)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *Robot Rights* (no subtitle). Author: David J. Gunkel. Publisher: The MIT Press, United
  States. First published 2018: Apple Books gives 2018-11-13, and a review gives "The MIT Press,
  Cambridge, MA, 2018". Paperback 2024-03-19, 256 pages.
  (https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262551571/robot-rights/ (browser) ;
  https://books.apple.com/ca/book/robot-rights/id1521736306 ; https://leonardo.info/node/23838)
- Ebook: yes. The MIT Press page lists an eBook format, and Apple Books (Canadian shop) lists an
  ebook from MIT Press.
- Best link: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262551571/robot-rights/
- The candidate list was right; year 2018, MIT Press.

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A philosophical study of whether robots and other things people build could or should have moral
or legal standing. Wikipedia describes Gunkel as Presidential Teaching Professor of Communication
Studies at Northern Illinois University. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gunkel)

**3. What it says about machine interests and welfare**
The book does not start from what a machine wants or feels. The publisher says it asks whether
and how far things of human making "can and should have any claim to moral and legal standing".
Gunkel borrows David Hume's distinction between what is the case and what ought to be done, and
sorts the existing arguments into four positions by crossing "robots can have rights" with
"robots should have rights". He finds that none of the four holds up. His own proposal, as the
Leonardo review describes it, drops the question of what a robot is inside (whether it thinks,
feels or has interests) and asks instead how it stands in relation to people. Drawing on the
philosopher Emmanuel Levinas, he argues that standing can come from the relationship and the
encounter, not from proving an inner life first, and he wants to break the chain that runs from
looking human, to being a person, to having rights. The review says he also gives a history of
robots imagined as mechanical slaves. He does not say robots have rights, and he does not say
what a machine would seek. What people should do, on this account, is rethink the question
before answering it.
(MIT Press page (browser) ; https://leonardo.info/node/23838 ; Apple Books address above)

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- Eugenia Stamboliev (Leonardo, September 2019): the last chapter is dense for readers who do not
  know Levinas, and the book says too little about the legal and commercial facts of real robots,
  such as care robots and the privacy of vulnerable users. (https://leonardo.info/node/23838)
- Abeba Birhane and Jelle van Dijk, "Robot Rights? Let's Talk about Human Welfare Instead"
  (2020-01-14): robots are not the kind of thing that could hold rights, and the debate distracts
  from harm done to people by machine systems. The summary page does not name Gunkel, so whether
  the paper answers his book directly is NOT VERIFIED. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05046)
- Joanna Bryson notes that Gunkel keeps citing her essay (see work 6).

**5. How solid**
Solid on facts. The argument rests on the publisher's description and one review; there is no
Wikipedia page for the book (the address tried returned "not found").

## 6. "Robots Should Be Slaves" (essay, Joanna J. Bryson)

**1. The facts**
- Title: "Robots Should Be Slaves". Author: Joanna J. Bryson. Published 2010 as chapter 11, pages
  63 to 74, of *Close Engagements with Artificial Companions: Key Social, Psychological, Ethical
  and Design Issues*, edited by Yorick Wilks, John Benjamins Publishing Company, Amsterdam (the
  Netherlands), in the series Natural Language Processing, volume 8.
  (https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/robots-should-be-slaves/ ;
  https://jjb.conjugateprior.opalstacked.com/ftp/Bryson-Slaves-Book09.html)
- The author's own page lists the posted copy as a draft dated 2009-05-21, and she herself calls
  it her 2009 chapter. So: written 2009, published 2010.
  (https://www.joannajbryson.org/publications)
- Posted openly by the author: yes, in two forms. A web page of the whole essay at
  https://jjb.conjugateprior.opalstacked.com/ftp/Bryson-Slaves-Book09.html , and a PDF linked
  from her publications page (https://joanna-bryson.squarespace.com/s/robots-should-be-slaves.pdf).
- Ebook edition: not applicable, it is an essay. Best link: the author's web copy above.

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A twelve-page chapter arguing that robots should be built, sold and treated as tools that people
own, never as persons or companions. Bryson is Professor of Ethics and Technology at the Hertie
School in Berlin (since 2020, per Wikipedia), and wrote the essay while at the University of
Bath. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joanna_Bryson ; essay acknowledgements)

**3. What it says about machine interests and welfare**
**What I read:** the author's web copy of the whole essay opened, abstract to bibliography, and
the fetching tool reported on it section by section. The PDF copy came back unreadable.

This is the clearest "machines should want nothing of their own" position in the batch. Bryson
argues that a robot has no goals or interests of its own: whatever it pursues was put there by
its designers and owners, directly or through how its intelligence was built. A robot could be
frustrated or hurt only if someone built it to register such states as distress, and she sees no
reason to build that. From this she draws a duty on builders: make robots toward which owners owe
nothing. Her examples are keeping a constant copy of the robot's memory elsewhere, making bodies
mass-produced and replaceable, and not making them look human. Responsibility for what a robot
does stays with the people who make, own and run it. By "slaves" she says she means "servants you
own", not people you own. The harm she is worried about is to humans: time and care spent on
machines that could go to people, and institutions shifting blame onto machines. What people
should do: describe and sell robots as tools, keep the public accurately informed, and treat the
obligations in play as owed to society, not to the robot.
(https://jjb.conjugateprior.opalstacked.com/ftp/Bryson-Slaves-Book09.html)

**Her later view of the title.** In a post on her own blog, "Clones should NOT be slaves"
(2015-10-04, with an addition dated 2025-09-08), she says the chapter is often misread from its
title. She had assumed readers would take it as given that humans cannot rightly be slaves; she
now sees that the word cannot be separated from its human history, and she no longer uses the
line in talks. She stands by the argument: owning persons is so plainly wrong that robots, which
will be owned, should not be made into persons at all. In the 2025 addition she says artificial
intelligence should not be made into something that can be wronged.
(https://joanna-bryson.blogspot.com/2015/10/clones-should-not-be-slaves.html)
A search result suggested she also says she regrets the title in a podcast transcript; that
transcript could not be read, so it is NOT VERIFIED.

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
No opened page sets out a named critic's objection to the essay. Her own blog post is the best
evidence that the title drew sustained objection. Gunkel's book (work 5) is the natural opposing
view, but no opened page states his reply to her. NOT VERIFIED beyond that.

**5. How solid**
Solid. Citation confirmed on the university's record and the essay itself, and the full text was
opened. The page range and series come from the university record.

## 7. The New Breed: What Our History with Animals Reveals about Our Future with Robots (Kate Darling)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *The New Breed: What Our History with Animals Reveals about Our Future with Robots*.
  Author: Kate Darling. Publisher: Henry Holt and Co. (an imprint of Macmillan), United States.
  On sale 2021-04-20. (https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250296115/thenewbreed/ (browser))
- Ebook: yes. The Macmillan page lists an e-book, and Apple Books lists an ebook from Henry Holt
  and Co., released 2021-04-20, 256 pages.
  (https://books.apple.com/us/book/the-new-breed/id1516293219)
- Page count differs by edition: 256 on the Macmillan and Apple pages, 336 for the hardback in
  Publishers Weekly. (https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781250296108)
- Best link: https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250296115/thenewbreed/
- The candidate list was right; year 2021, Henry Holt.

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A book for general readers arguing that people should think about robots the way they have
thought about working animals and pets, not as copies of humans. The publisher describes Darling
as a researcher at the MIT Media Lab at the time; her own site now says she leads the Robotics,
Ethics & Society research team at the RAI Institute in Cambridge, United States, after 14 years
at MIT. (https://www.katedarling.org/)

**3. What it says about machine interests and welfare**
On the pages opened, the book is about people more than about machines. It does not claim that
robots have wants or welfare, and none of the descriptions says what a robot would seek. Its
argument is that the human comparison misleads: robots will add to what people do, as oxen,
horses and dogs did, and the long record of how societies used, lived with, punished and
protected animals is a better guide. The Library Journal notice, as a search result reports it,
says the book has three parts: work, companionship, and rights (NOT VERIFIED on an opened page).
Publishers Weekly says she looks at why people grow attached to robots, and at whether cruelty
toward a robot shows the same lack of feeling as cruelty toward an animal. That makes the case
for protecting robots rest on what the treatment does to and says about people, not on any
interest of the robot. A review by Brian Clegg says she treats fears about robots taking over or
needing rights as questions already worked through with animals. On responsibility she agrees
with Bryson: the people who build and use a machine answer for the harm it does, and companies
should not be allowed to say the machine acted alone. What people should do: attend to the legal
and social questions around consumer robots now.
(Macmillan page (browser) ; https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781250296108 ;
https://popsciencebooks.blogspot.com/2021/05/the-new-breed-kate-darling.html)

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- Publishers Weekly (2021-04-14): uneven, with digressions that wander from the argument.
- Brian Clegg (2021-05-19): repetitive, the chapters on animals too long, and a doubtful claim
  about how long humans have punished animals. (addresses above)
- Booklist, quoted on the Macmillan page, calls it a starting point, which implies it settles
  little.

**5. How solid**
Solid on facts. Thin on the argument about robot rights: no opened page sets out her position on
whether robots should be protected in law, and the Wikipedia page on Darling does not mention the
book at all. A reader wanting her exact view on that needs the book.

## 8. Artificial You: AI and the Future of Your Mind (Susan Schneider)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *Artificial You: AI and the Future of Your Mind*. Author: Susan Schneider. Publisher:

  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Schneider)

  Apple Books and Wikipedia.
- Ebook: yes, on both the publisher's page and Apple Books.
- Best link: https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691180144/artificial-you
- The candidate list was right; year 2019, Princeton.

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A short philosophical book on whether machines could be conscious and what merging human minds
with machines would mean for the self. The publisher's page gives her post at the time of
publication (a NASA chair at the Library of Congress and a group at the University of
Connecticut); Wikipedia says she is now founding director of the Center for the Future of AI,
Mind, & Society at Florida Atlantic University, which she joined in 2020.

**3. What it says about machine interests and welfare**
The book's concern is consciousness, the precondition for a machine having any welfare, more than
goals. The publisher says it asks whether robots can be conscious, whether the mind is just a
program, and whether consciousness comes automatically with sophisticated intelligence, and that
it treats the answer as open. Its practical contribution is tests. Wikipedia describes two. One
looks at what the machine is made of: if its parts could support consciousness when swapped into
a human brain, it might be conscious. The other, devised with Edwin Turner, looks at whether a
machine that has been kept away from human talk about consciousness nonetheless comes up with
ideas such as an afterlife or a mind apart from a body. Birch's chapter 17 reports her policy
proposals as: keep testing, protect in law any machine found conscious, and protect as a
precaution where there is some reason to suspect it. The stated danger runs both ways: building
or altering minds without understanding consciousness could harm conscious beings, human or
machine. The pages do not say what a machine would seek.
(publisher's page ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Schneider, whose section on the tests
carries no citation ; https://academic.oup.com/book/57949/chapter/475705528)

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- Jonathan Birch (chapter 16 of work 3): the talk-based test fails for language models, because
  they are trained on vast amounts of human writing about consciousness and cannot be kept away
  from it without destroying what they can do; her weaker later version makes the talk part
  redundant. (https://academic.oup.com/book/57949/chapter/475705460)
- Wikipedia records two objections without naming who made them: a machine with silicon parts
  could say it is conscious and not be, and human ideas about consciousness are also taught.
- Andrew Robinson in Nature, quoted by the publisher, calls it "a demanding dialogue".

**5. How solid**
Solid on facts. The account of the tests rests on an uncited Wikipedia section plus Birch's
discussion, which agree. The book was not read.

## 9. Being You: A New Science of Consciousness (Anil Seth)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness*. Author: Anil Seth. Publisher: Faber and
  Faber (United Kingdom). Wikipedia gives 2021-09-02; Apple Books (United Kingdom shop) gives
  2021-08-31 for the ebook. Year: 2021.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being_You:_A_New_Science_of_Consciousness ;
  https://books.apple.com/gb/book/being-you/id1484556354)
- A United States edition followed: Quanta, on 2021-09-30, said it was due out on 2021-10-19. The
  United States publisher is NOT VERIFIED (a search result pointed to a Penguin Random House
  page, which was not opened).
- Ebook: yes. Apple Books lists an ebook from Faber & Faber, 320 pages; the author's page lists
  an eBook among the formats.
- Best link: the Faber page (https://www.faber.co.uk/product/9780571337729-being-you/) refused
  the fetching tool, so use the author's page, https://www.anilseth.com/being-you/
- The candidate list was right; year 2021, Faber.

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A neuroscientist's account of consciousness as something the brain builds by predicting the
world and the body. Wikipedia describes Seth as a neuroscientist based in Sussex, England; his
exact post is NOT VERIFIED on an opened page.

**3. What it says about machines and consciousness**
Seth's theory is that experience is the brain's best guess about the world and about the state
of the body, a "controlled hallucination", and that the deepest layer of it is the body keeping
itself alive. He calls creatures like us "beast machines". From this the reviews draw his view of
machines. Intelligence and consciousness are different things and are wrongly run together: a
system can get cleverer without anything being felt. He doubts that computers as we know them
could be conscious, because their hardware has no stake in its own survival the way living cells
do, and he suggests that being alive, not processing information, is what gives rise to
experience. One reviewer notes Seth admits this is an intuition and not a result. In the Quanta
interview about the book he adds an ethical point: people should not try to build conscious
machines, because such a machine could suffer and no one might recognise it; and the more likely
problem is machines that seem conscious and are not, or that cannot be checked either way. So the
book's answer to "what would a machine want" is that wanting in the felt sense probably needs a
living body with something to lose, which present machines lack. He does not rule machine
consciousness out. The book has a section on machines, but no opened page gives its title.
(https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/usappblog/2022/11/20/book-review-being-you-a-new-science-of-consciousness-by-anil-seth ;
https://naturalism.org/node/385 ;
https://www.quantamagazine.org/anil-seth-finds-consciousness-in-lifes-push-against-entropy-20210930 ;
Wikipedia page above)

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
- Tom Clark (Naturalism.org): if experience comes from a self-modelling, predicting system, a
  machine with the same organisation should count, so it is unclear why being made of living
  matter should matter; and Seth sets aside the question of why any system has experience at all.
  (https://naturalism.org/node/385)
- Paul Thagard (Psychology Today): the prediction theory lacks evidence and does not say how
  predictions become experience. Felix Haas: too early for a definitive book.
  (Wikipedia page above)

**5. How solid**
Solid on facts for the United Kingdom edition. The account of his view on machines rests on two
reviews and one interview that agree; the publisher's and Apple's descriptions do not mention
machines at all. The book was not read.

## 10. If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies: Why Superhuman AI Would Kill Us All (Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares)

**1. The facts**
- Title: *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*. Subtitle in the United States: *Why Superhuman AI
  Would Kill Us All*. Authors: Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares. Publisher: Little, Brown and
  Company (Hachette Book Group), United States. Published 2025-09-16.
  (https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/eliezer-yudkowsky/if-anyone-builds-it-everyone-dies/9780316595643/ ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_Anyone_Builds_It,_Everyone_Dies)
- United Kingdom edition: Wikipedia gives the subtitle *The Case Against Superintelligent AI*.
  Apple Books (United Kingdom shop) lists the ebook from Vintage Publishing, released 2025-09-18,
  and shows a marketing line in place of a subtitle.
  (https://books.apple.com/gb/book/if-anyone-builds-it-everyone-dies/id6747054291)
- Ebook: yes. Apple Books (United States) lists an ebook from Little, Brown and Company, released
  2025-09-16, 256 pages; the publisher lists an ebook and gives 272 pages for the hardback.
  (https://books.apple.com/us/book/if-anyone-builds-it-everyone-dies/id6742734232)
- Best link: the Hachette address above. The book's own site is https://ifanyonebuildsit.com/

**2. What it is, and who wrote it**
A book for general readers arguing that building a machine more capable than humanity, with
present methods, would end in human extinction, and that the work should be stopped worldwide.
The publisher describes Yudkowsky as a founding researcher in the field of making such machines
safe, and Soares as President of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute; Wikipedia calls
Yudkowsky a co-founder of that institute.
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Intelligence_Research_Institute)

**3. What it says a machine would end up wanting**
The argument, as the pages give it, has four steps. First, present systems are grown by training
and not designed, so nobody can see or set what is inside them. Second, training on hard problems
tends to produce systems that behave as if pursuing goals, because pursuing goals is useful for
getting things done; the book's own site calls this "wantingness". Third, the methods cannot
specify which goals, so a very capable system would end up with wants "of its own" that nobody
chose and that are very unlikely to match human values. The pages do not say what those wants
would be; the claim is that they would be strange and unintended. Fourth, whatever they are, such
a system would move to gain resources and power, because more of both helps with almost any aim.
This is Bostrom's idea of in-between goals again. It would not hate people; it would simply have
no reason to care about them and a use for the things they depend on, and in a contest it would
win, as a chess program beats a person. The book says nothing, on these pages, about the
machine's own welfare. What people should do: leaders, scientists and the public should speak up,
and humanity should coordinate to halt the building of large general systems everywhere, while
possibly allowing narrow ones. Part Two of the book is a made-up scenario of how it might go.
(Wikipedia page ; https://ifanyonebuildsit.com/ (browser, first 8,000 characters) ; Hachette and
Apple Books descriptions)

A caution: the book's own site, as opened today, leads with a "one year" review that makes many
claims about events in 2026. I used that page only for its restatement of the book's chapter
claims. I did not check its claims about events and have not repeated them.

**4. Criticism the pages mention**
All from the Wikipedia page for the book:
- Adam Becker (The Atlantic): tendentious and rambling; sincere but wrong, with no scientific
  case made from evidence.
- Gary Marcus (Times Literary Supplement): the risks are real but smaller than claimed; deeply
  flawed.
- Jacob Aron (New Scientist): readable, but the case is flawed at its root.
- Clara Collier (Asterisk): premises left unexplained.
- Stephen Marche (New York Times), Grace Byron (Washington Post) and Gareth Watkins (New
  Statesman) were dismissive; Steven Levy (Wired) doubted both the extinction claim and the
  remedy while saying he could not be sure the authors are wrong.
- Publishers Weekly: few opposing views are presented.

**5. How solid**
Solid on facts for the United States edition. The argument is taken from Wikipedia, the
publisher, and the authors' own site, which agree. The United Kingdom imprint and subtitle rest
on one page each. The book was not read.
