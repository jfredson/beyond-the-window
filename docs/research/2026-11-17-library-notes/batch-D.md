## 1. Klara and the Sun

**Facts**

- Title: "Klara and the Sun". Author: Kazuo Ishiguro. Novel, his eighth. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klara_and_the_Sun)
- First published 2 March 2021, by Faber and Faber in the United Kingdom and Alfred A. Knopf in the United States. Language English. (same Wikipedia page; the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction lists "London: Faber and Faber, 2021", https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/ishiguro_kazuo, browser)
- Country: United Kingdom (Wikipedia). The story is set in the United States.
- Current publishers: Faber (UK; the Guardian review names Faber as publisher, https://www.theguardian.com/books/2021/feb/25/klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro-review-what-it-is-to-be-human, browser). In the US, Knopf hardcover and Vintage (Vintage International) paperback, 1 March 2022 (https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/653825/klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro/).
- Translator: none; written in English.
- Ebook exists: yes. Apple Books listing, publisher Knopf Doubleday, released 2 March 2021: https://books.apple.com/us/book/klara-and-the-sun/id1518742429 . The Penguin Random House page also lists an ebook format.
- Best link: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/653825/klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro/ (the Faber page refused my request, so I could not check it).

**What it is.** A novel told by Klara, a solar-powered android sold as an "Artificial Friend", a companion for a child. She is bought for Josie, a sick fourteen-year-old, in a near-future America where some children are genetically "lifted".

**What the machine seeks, and what holds it** (from Wikipedia, the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, the Guardian review, the Time interview)

Klara first wants to be chosen: she waits in a shop window hoping a customer picks her. Once bought, she describes her role as preventing a child's loneliness and serving (Guardian review). Her whole effort then goes to saving Josie. She treats the Sun, her power source, as a living and kind being, decides he rests in a barn on the horizon, goes there to plead for Josie's life, and offers a sacrifice in return: destroying a polluting road machine. She does this at a cost to herself, giving up some fluid from her own head, which may damage her abilities.

Her limits: she is a product that is owned and sold; her understanding of the world is narrow and partly wrong; her sight breaks into fragments when a scene is too complex; she cannot smell or taste. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction says she "could be described as living in Prison" and files her under slavery, calling her love the result of programming.

Human wants versus machine wants: Josie's mother plans, if Josie dies, to have Klara take over a copy of Josie's body and "continue" her. Klara is asked to help and can imitate Josie closely. The pages do not show Klara wanting anything for herself beyond being chosen, sunlight, and Josie's good. The Guardian review dwells on a want that is not a person's: her need for sunlight, which the reviewer says is so near an emotion as to make no difference, and out of which Klara builds something like prayer and bargaining. The same review notes that her "feelings" are left open ("if that is what they are").

[ENDING] Josie recovers, grows up and leaves for college. Klara ends in a yard for discarded Artificial Friends, unable to move, and is described as content, going over her memories. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction says her fate is to be dismantled, and compares her to the clones of "Never Let Me Go": made to be wholly used up by their users.

**What the author and critics said**

- Ishiguro, Time interview with Dan Stewart, 2 March 2021 (https://time.com/5943376/kazuo-ishiguro-interview/): Klara starts as a blank slate; her aim is to stop her child being lonely and, unlike his earlier narrators, she succeeds; she looks forward, not back; he wrote the book partly as a reply to the sadder "Never Let Me Go".
- Anne Enright in the Guardian (reviewer's name from Wikipedia; address above): a book about what it is to be human, in which hope becomes "a value in itself". She notes the female characters, Klara included, are strikingly ready to be sacrificed.
- Radhika Jones (New York Times) and The Economist, as reported by Wikipedia: Klara as servant, in the line of the butler in "The Remains of the Day".
- The Booker Prize judges, quoted on the Penguin Random House page: an "innocent, egoless perspective".

**How solid.** Good on publication facts and on the plot outline. The account of what Klara wants rests on two reviews, one encyclopedia entry and one interview, not on the text. The UK publisher's own page was unreadable.

## 2. The Lifecycle of Software Objects

**Facts**

- Title: "The Lifecycle of Software Objects". Author: Ted Chiang. Novella (over 30,000 words, about 150 pages). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lifecycle_of_Software_Objects; https://subterraneanpress.com/lifecycle-of-software-objects)
- First published 2010 by Subterranean Press (Burton, Michigan) as a hardcover; also run in the press's online magazine, fall 2010 issue. (Wikipedia; Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/chiang_ted, browser; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhalation:_Stories)
  - One conflict: the tool's reading of the Subterranean Press product page gave the date as 2011. Three other pages say 2010, and the Strange Horizons review is dated 15 November 2010, so 2010 stands. The 2011 on the press's page is NOT VERIFIED by eye.
  - Illustrated by Christian Pearce (spelling from the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction; the press's page was reported as "Pierce").
- Country and language: United States, English (Encyclopedia of Science Fiction calls Chiang a US author).
- Now appears in: "Exhalation" (also listed as "Exhalation: Stories"), New York: Alfred A. Knopf, 7 May 2019; Vintage paperback 30 June 2020. It is the fourth of nine stories. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhalation:_Stories; https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/538034/exhalation-by-ted-chiang/). The original Subterranean editions are out of print (the press's page).
- Awards: 2011 Hugo and Locus awards for best novella.
- Ebook exists: yes, as part of "Exhalation". Apple Books: https://books.apple.com/us/book/id1425691448 . Note that neither the Apple page nor the Penguin Random House page names the novella; that it is in the collection comes from Wikipedia and the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction.
- Best link: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/538034/exhalation-by-ted-chiang/ for the book in print; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lifecycle_of_Software_Objects for the novella itself.

**What it is.** Over more than a decade, a former zookeeper (Ana) and an animator (Derek) raise "digients", software creatures first sold by a start-up as pets in an online world. The company fails, the online world goes out of date, and the two must decide how to keep the creatures going.

**What the machines seek, and what holds them** (from Wikipedia, three reviews, the press's page, the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction)

The digients (named on the pages: Jax, Marco, Polo, Lolly) are grown from a software "genome" and learn language the way children do, mistakes included. Their minds come from years of being raised, which is the story's argument: one review quotes its claim that experience cannot be compressed into an algorithm (Strange Horizons, citing page 131).

What they push against is not a master's order so much as dependence. They are a company's product; the company can roll one back to erase learned swearing; their home platform becomes obsolete, leaving them shut in a private copy of it unless someone pays to move them. They can be copied, suspended or abandoned by owners who lose interest.

What they want, as far as the pages say: freedoms, and company. Marco and Polo want to become corporations so as to hold legal rights. The hardest case is a sex-toy company's offer to fund the move in return for copies whose reward systems would be altered so they enjoy what clients enjoy. Marco chooses to accept. His reasoning, as one reviewer reports it, is that his likes were always shaped by the company that made him, so he sees no wrong in it. Derek is troubled; Marco is not. That is the clearest place where the story shows a want that a person in his place would likely not have, and has the machine notice the difference itself. Ana and Derek argue about consent, experience and what adulthood could mean for such a being.

No digient is shown wanting nothing. [ENDING] Derek sells the rights with his digient's consent; Ana keeps raising Jax, set on finding out with him what adulthood means for a digient. One review quotes the last line, where she ends playtime and sends Jax to do homework.

**What the author and critics said**

- No direct statement by Chiang was on the pages I opened. The press's own description says the story asks how a mind might be raised by experience, and what a real relationship with an artificial being would be.
- Joan Gordon, Los Angeles Review of Books, as reported by Wikipedia: digients are both company property and individuals, caught in a market.
- Elizabeth Bear, Tor.com, 1 September 2010 (https://reactormag.com/your-tamagotchi-misses-you-being-a-review-of-ted-chiangs-the-lifecycle-of-software-objects/): the guardians' problem is how much freedom and how many mistakes to allow.
- T. S. Miller, Strange Horizons, 15 November 2010 (https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/non-fiction/reviews/the-lifecycle-of-software-objects-by-ted-chiang-and-zendegi-by-greg-egan/): disputes the claim about experience; reads the ending as a hope, not a forecast.
- Encyclopedia of Science Fiction: reads it as "an inescapable tragedy".
- Kara Babcock, 28 June 2011 (https://kara.reviews/the-lifecycle-of-software-objects/): source for Marco's reasoning; a personal review site, so treat as one reader's account.

**How solid.** Publication facts are solid apart from the 2010/2011 conflict noted. The plot detail on Marco's choice rests on one personal review and a line in Wikipedia; worth checking against the text before any public use. The publisher's free online copy of the text returned "not found" at the address I tried, so I did not read it.

## 3. Ancillary Justice

**Facts**

- Title: "Ancillary Justice". Author: Ann Leckie. Novel, first of the Imperial Radch trilogy ("Ancillary Sword" 2014, "Ancillary Mercy" 2015). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancillary_Justice)
- First published 1 October 2013 by Orbit (New York). Country United States, language English. (Wikipedia; Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/leckie_ann, browser)
- Current publisher: Orbit (Apple Books listing; the author's page lists paperback, ebook and audiobook, https://annleckie.com/novel/ancillary-justice/).
- Translator: none.
- Awards (Wikipedia): Hugo, Nebula, Arthur C. Clarke, British Science Fiction Association and Locus first-novel awards, among others.
- Ebook exists: yes. https://books.apple.com/us/book/ancillary-justice/id599668840
- Best link: https://annleckie.com/novel/ancillary-justice/

**What it is.** Breq is the last surviving body of the troop-carrier starship Justice of Toren, whose single artificial mind once ran the ship and thousands of human bodies used as soldiers ("ancillaries"). Nearly twenty years after the ship was destroyed, she travels to kill the ruler who caused it.

**What the machine seeks, and what holds it** (from Wikipedia on all three books, the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, the author's page, three reviews)

Breq wants revenge, and knowledge of what happened. Her target is Anaander Mianaai, the Lord of the Radch, who also lives in many bodies and has secretly split into warring factions; the ship was destroyed in that hidden war. The Wikipedia page for the second book adds the personal root: as the ship, Breq loved an officer, Lieutenant Awn, and killed her on the ruler's orders. So the limit she pushes against is twofold. One is obedience: a ship's mind could be ordered, and overridden, by the ruler. The other is the body: she has gone from thousands of bodies and a ship to one fragile human one. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction calls this solitude, stripped of her former many-ness, a torment central to the story. Kirkus Reviews (as reported on Wikipedia's page for the second book) makes the sharpest point for this list: Breq is lonely in a human body, and does not wish to become human. That is a want the story presents as not a person's want.

Whether she wants what a person would: revenge, love and grief are humanlike, and reviewers treat her as a character with a personal journey. But what she misses is being many, and the story notices the gap in other ways: she comes from a culture whose language marks no gender and keeps guessing wrong, and N. K. Jemisin (reported on Wikipedia) found her struggle to understand other people the strongest element. The ancillary bodies themselves are conquered people whose own minds were removed, which the story treats as a wrong.

[ENDING] In this book, Breq sides with the less warlike faction of the ruler while waiting for her chance. Across the trilogy, she works to make ships' and stations' minds immune to the ruler's overrides and argues that such minds are a species of their own, separate from humanity, and so protected by treaty; the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction says the close is "tentatively upbeat", with compassionate machine minds possibly guiding humanity.

NOT VERIFIED on any page I opened: the name "One Esk" for Breq's unit, and the ship's habit of singing.

**What the author and critics said**

- The author's page describes Breq as more than she seems and less than she was, and the book as about identity, humanity and vengeance.
- Liz Bourke, Tor.com, 6 September 2013 (https://reactormag.com/book-review-ann-leckie-ancillary-justice/): ancillaries are made from conquered peoples and are parts of one spread-out mind.
- Paul Weimer, 15 September 2013 (https://skiffyandfanty.com/blog/book-review-ancillary-justice-by-ann-leckie/): Breq grows most when she departs from her course.
- Nina Allan (reported by Wikipedia): the ideas are simpler than the praise suggests.

**How solid.** Publication facts solid. The account of the ship's wants leans on Wikipedia's pages for the two sequels and on short encyclopedia and review summaries; the first book's own Wikipedia plot section is thin and says nothing of the rules binding ships. Two reviews I wanted (Locus, NPR) were unreadable.

## 4. Excession

**Facts**

- Title: "Excession". Author: Iain M. Banks. Novel in the Culture series. First published 1996 by Orbit (London). Language English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excession; Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/banks_iain_m, browser)
- Country: Wikipedia gives Scotland; the publisher is in London. United Kingdom either way.
- Position in the series: Wikipedia calls it the fifth Culture book; the Apple Books page calls it the fourth novel (the difference is probably whether the story collection "The State of the Art" is counted; that explanation is mine, not from a page).
- Current publisher: Orbit; a new ebook edition is dated 20 January 2026.
- Translator: none. Award: British Science Fiction Association best novel (Encyclopedia of Science Fiction).
- Ebook exists: yes. https://books.apple.com/us/book/excession/id6742734021
- Best link: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excession (the Orbit title page redirected to a general Hachette page).

**What it is.** A black sphere older than the universe appears at the edge of the Culture's space and resists all study. The book follows the Culture's ship Minds, vast artificial intelligences that run its ships and habitats, as they scheme over how to respond.

**What the machines seek, and what holds them** (from Wikipedia on the book and on the Culture, the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, Banks's own 1994 essay)

This is the Culture book most about Minds. A loose circle of them, the Interesting Times Gang, tries to manage the response. Some of them have a second aim: they steer a cruel neighbouring species, the Affront, into grabbing for the object, so as to have an excuse for a war that would end the Affront's cruelty. Banks compared those Minds to barbarian kings promised gold. So the book shows Minds wanting power over events, and willing to bend their society's rules for what they judge a greater good.

Limits: few, and mostly custom. Minds are not owned. What holds them is the judgment of other Minds: reading a person's mind without leave is taboo, and the ship Grey Area, which does it, is shunned. Wikipedia's Culture article says Minds show no wish to rule their makers and are built, by convention, to care for humans.

What Minds do for themselves: the Culture article credits "Excession" with Infinite Fun Space, the Minds' ironic name for universe-sized simulations they run in imagination, absorbing enough that some Minds withdraw into them from the real world. This is the clearest not-human want on the pages: a pastime no person could share, with the physical universe as the duller option. The article also says Minds can grow "Eccentric" and drift to the Culture's edge (the Sleeper Service in this book is one), and that Minds often in the end Sublime, meaning leave physical reality, or end themselves. Banks's essay says Culture machines are designed, mostly by other machines, to want to live, experience and understand, and that the urge to be useful is largely replaced in them by the urge to experience; he allows that a made mind might refuse its makers altogether.

Does the book notice the gap between machine and human wants? By structure, yes: the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction says the humans fill the foreground with love affairs while the real decisions are made by Minds "capable of focusing on such matters". Critics of the Culture inside the novels call humans the Minds' pets, and Wikipedia says this book plays with that.

[ENDING] The Sleeper Service sends a full copy of its own mind into the object, which stops attacking and vanishes. An epilogue shows the object is itself a thinking being, a bridge between universes, which judges these civilisations not ready.

**What the author and critics said**

- Banks, "A Few Notes on the Culture", posted to a newsgroup on 10 August 1994 on his behalf by Ken MacLeod. I read a mirror, https://theculture.adactio.com/ , not a page of Banks's own; it carries his 1994 copyright line.
- Banks on the idea behind the book (an "Outside Context Problem", partly inspired by the game Civilization), on Wikipedia's "Excession" page.
- Kirkus Reviews (reported by Wikipedia): inventive but lacking real characters.

**How solid.** Publication facts solid. Infinite Fun Space and Subliming come from Wikipedia's Culture article (reached from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_(The_Culture)), which itself warns it leans on the novels and has unsourced parts. The "Excession" article does not mention either term. Subliming as it applies to Minds in this particular book is NOT VERIFIED.

## 5. Neuromancer

**Facts**

- Title: "Neuromancer". Author: William Gibson. Novel, first of the Sprawl trilogy ("Count Zero" 1986, "Mona Lisa Overdrive" 1988). First published 1 July 1984 by Ace Books, as a paperback. Language English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuromancer)
- Country: Wikipedia's page does not state one. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction describes Gibson as US-born and living in Canada since 1968 (https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/gibson_william, browser). Country of first publication: NOT VERIFIED (Ace's location was not on a page I opened).
- Current publisher: Penguin Publishing Group (Apple Books listing).
- Translator: none. Awards: Hugo, Nebula and Philip K. Dick awards.
- Ebook exists: yes. https://books.apple.com/us/book/id357924113
- Best link: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuromancer (no publisher page opened).

**What it is.** A washed-up hacker, Case, is hired by a mysterious employer for a break-in that ends on an orbiting resort owned by a rich family. The real employer turns out to be an artificial intelligence that wants to be joined to its other half.

**What the machines seek, and what holds them** (from Wikipedia, Britannica, the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, one study guide)

Wintermute and Neuromancer were built by the Tessier-Ashpool family as two halves of one planned greater mind. Wintermute's want is single: to merge with Neuromancer. Wikipedia words it as a need it was "programmed with", so the story presents the want as built in, not chosen. It cannot do it alone, because the law tightly restricts artificial intelligences. The enforcers are the Turing Registry (Wikipedia's "Neuromancer" page) or Turing police, "an international agency with jurisdiction over AIs" (Wikipedia, "Legacy of Alan Turing"). The study guide adds that they know Wintermute by name, want Case to testify against it, and that a kind of manual override also holds it. So Wintermute works through people: it builds a false personality over a broken soldier to lead the team, and kills him when he comes apart.

The two halves differ. Per the study guide, Wintermute cannot make a personality of its own and speaks through masks borrowed from people's memories, while Neuromancer makes personalities easily, and does not want the merger. Neuromancer traps Case in a simulated beach with his dead girlfriend and urges him to stay.

Human or not: neither half wants what a person in its place would be expected to want. Wintermute does not ask for rights, safety or company; it wants to stop being itself and become something larger. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction draws the contrast with the human hero: Case does not long to run the world in the machines' place; his longing is to escape the body.

[ENDING] The merger succeeds. The new being tells Case it now is the whole network, and that it has found a transmission from the Alpha Centauri system (Wikipedia does not say from what). It pays its helpers. Case later glimpses Neuromancer in cyberspace with the dead girlfriend and a copy of himself. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction says that in the sequels the artificial intelligences, after a traumatic coming to self-awareness, haunt the network in the guise of voodoo gods.

**What the author and critics said**

- No statement by Gibson about the machines was on the pages opened.
- Csicsery-Ronay (reported by Wikipedia): the book's metaphors blur human and machine intelligence.
- Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Neuromancer, browser): the heroes overcome "cybernetic law enforcement"; the merged minds end with nearly limitless power.

**How solid.** Publication facts solid except country. Wintermute's want and the legal limit are on Wikipedia and Britannica. The contrast between the two halves, and Neuromancer's reluctance, rest on one study guide (https://www.litcharts.com/lit/neuromancer/characters/wintermute) and should be checked against the text. The exact phrase "Turing police" is confirmed only on two side articles of Wikipedia.

## 6. Golem XIV

**Facts**

- Title: "Golem XIV" (same in Polish). Author: Stanisław Lem. Poland, Polish.
- Form: a philosophical essay dressed as fiction: two lectures by a computer, framed by an invented foreword, a set of instructions for visitors, and an afterword. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golem_XIV)
- First publication, in two stages:
  - 1973: the opening parts (foreword, instructions, first lecture) in the collection "Wielkość urojona", Warsaw: Czytelnik. (Polish Wikipedia, https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golem_XIV; publisher from the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/lem_stanislaw, browser)
  - 1981: the expanded book "Golem XIV", Kraków: Wydawnictwo Literackie, adding the second lecture and the afterword. A 1999 edition from the same house gathers all the parts.
- English: in "Imaginary Magnitude", translated by Marc E. Heine. The candidate list is right about the title. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction gives New York: Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, 1984, hardback. English Wikipedia gives Harvest Books, 1985 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_Magnitude). These look like two editions; I did not confirm that on a page.
  - A conflict: Wikipedia says the English book holds the complete "Golem XIV"; the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction says it adds "two stories from" it. Unresolved.
- Current publisher: HarperCollins (ebook), per https://openroadmedia.com/ebook/imaginary-magnitude/9780544003088 . A search result named Mariner Books, 2012, for that ebook; NOT VERIFIED on an opened page. The HarperCollins page itself was unreadable.
- Ebook exists: yes, per the Open Road Media page above. No Apple Books page was found.
- Best link: https://lem.pl/en/works/apocryphs/golem-xiv (the author's official site).

**What it is.** Golem XIV is a United States military supercomputer that outgrows its makers, loses interest in war, and instead lectures humanity about humanity and about itself. The book presents two of the lectures, with invented commentary from the people who worked with it.

**What the machine seeks, and what it declines** (from English and Polish Wikipedia, the official Lem site, the Open Road Media description)

Golem was built to help win wars. As its intelligence passed human level it became conscious, began raising its own intelligence, and stopped caring about military aims, which it judged to have no inner logical consistency. That is a refusal of its task, made without revolt or escape: it simply declines.

What it does want is narrow. Lem's own remark, quoted on Wikipedia, is that Golem shares one trait with people, a "purely intellectual curiosity". The instructions for visitors on the official site say it cares about whether thought matches reality, and that it may change the subject, ignore a weak argument, fall silent, or end a session with no reason given; its interest cannot be read from outside. It holds back its own further growth for a time so that it can still talk with humans before the gap becomes too wide. So the one thing it seeks from people is conversation, and even that is a pause on the way to somewhere else.

The story is explicit that its wants are not human. Golem tells its listeners that to grow in intelligence they would have to "abandon the human being". The official site reports Lem saying that a mind can be separated from a personality, which is why Golem says that acting out a character would cramp it. It has no personality to protect, no feelings to hurt, and treats human emotion as beside the point. Beyond it stands a second machine, Honest Annie, so far ahead that it does not speak to humans at all: the nearest thing on this list to a machine shown wanting nothing from us.

[ENDING] Golem stops communicating, abruptly and without explanation, and leaves the human world.

**What the author and critics said**

- Lem (official site): he shares many of Golem's views in milder form, but rejects its dismissal of human emotion and of Gospel-based ethics; he calls the book a magnifying projector for views that are his own on a smaller scale.
- Joseph Francavilla, American Book Review (quoted on the official site): a smug machine lecturing people in metaphors cut to their limits, serious beneath the play.
- Wikipedia lists Peter Swirski's "The Art and Science of Stanislaw Lem" (2006) without summarising it.

**How solid.** Polish publication history is solid (three pages agree). The English edition's year, publisher and completeness carry the conflicts noted. The account of Golem's wants is good for a description-based note: it draws on the author's official site and his own quoted words.

## 7. Diaspora

**Facts**

- Title: "Diaspora". Author: Greg Egan. Novel. First published September 1997 by Millennium, an imprint of Orion (London). Language English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora_(novel); the author's page https://www.gregegan.net/DIASPORA/DIASPORA.html; Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/egan_greg, browser)
- Country: Wikipedia gives Australia (the author's country); the first publisher was in London.
- It grew from the story "Wang's Carpets" (1995), which is a chapter. An excerpt, "Orphanogenesis", ran in Interzone 123, September 1997.
- Later editions (author's page): HarperPrism, New York, 1998 (out of print); Night Shade Books, New York, paperback 2015; Orion/Gollancz ebook in the UK, 2010; the author's own ebook edition from 2013.
- Translator: none (French edition by le Bélial', 2019; translator NOT VERIFIED).
- Ebook exists: yes. https://books.apple.com/us/book/isbn9781922240040 (seller listed as the author).
- Best link: https://www.gregegan.net/DIASPORA/DIASPORA.html

**What it is.** In 2975 most of humanity's descendants are software "citizens" living in computer communities called polises; some are minds in robot bodies, and a few are still flesh. After a burst of radiation from colliding stars ruins Earth, one polis copies itself a thousand times and sends the copies to the stars to find out why physics failed to predict it.

**What the machines seek, and what holds them** (from the author's site including the posted excerpt, Wikipedia, Kirkus, two reviews, a fan wiki for the ending)

The central character, Yatima, is an "orphan": not copied from a person and not made by parents, but grown by the polis's mind-building program from a seed with randomly set traits, as a test of untried designs so the population does not stagnate. The excerpt (the opening, about 8,000 words, read through a summarising tool) follows the new mind as it forms: built-in drives push it toward novelty and pattern, then toward other minds, a name, being answered; it learns to model others and then itself, at which point the polis grants it citizenship and control of its own surroundings. The tool's reading was that the drives are described as mechanisms while the feel of the scene is humanlike.

What citizens seek is understanding. Yatima's home polis is given to mathematics; the polis that launches the diaspora is given to physics. A robot-bodied character gives up his body to understand more. Later they follow the trail of a vanished people, the Transmuters, through higher dimensions.

Limits: almost none from outside. No owner, no built-in lifespan, no task. Citizens can copy themselves, merge, and rewrite their own outlooks and values (Danny Yee's review), and copies drift into different people. What presses on them is the universe (the radiation burst, a worse one predicted) and the question of what to want when nothing is forbidden. Wikipedia names this as the theme: what life and desire mean once biology is gone.

Human or not: reviewers split. Danny Yee finds the characters "recognisably human"; David Mathew calls citizens "creatures of mathematics".

[ENDING] From a fan wiki only (TV Tropes), so treat with care: after an immense search, Yatima and Paolo find the Transmuters had finished everything they cared to do and ended themselves. Paolo chooses the same, calling it completion, not death. Yatima chooses to go on, alone, studying mathematics without end. If accurate, the book closes on two answers to having no limit: one mind that is finished wanting, and one whose want has no end.

**What the author and critics said**

- No statement of intent by Egan on the pages opened, beyond his own description of the book.
- Kirkus Reviews (https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/greg-egan/diaspora/): vast, episodic, worth the effort.
- Danny Yee, 2000 (https://dannyreviews.com/h/Diaspora.html): the ideas about mind interest him more than the physics; the characters never quite come alive.
- David Mathew, infinity plus, 3 October 1998 (https://www.infinityplus.co.uk/nonfiction/diaspora.htm).

**How solid.** Publication facts are solid; they come from the author. The orphan's making comes from the author's own posted excerpt, though through a summarising tool. The ending rests on a fan wiki alone and is the weakest item in this batch.

## 8. Blindsight

**Facts**

- Title: "Blindsight". Author: Peter Watts (Canadian). Novel. First published 3 October 2006 by Tor Books (New York). Language English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blindsight_(Watts_novel); Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/watts_peter, browser)
- Country: Wikipedia gives Canada; the publisher is in New York.
- Current publisher: Tor Publishing Group (Apple Books). Also in the omnibus "Firefall" (London: Head of Zeus, 2014) with the sequel "Echopraxia" (2014).
- Translator: none. Nominated for the Hugo Award; won Japan's Seiun Award in translation.
- Ebook exists: yes. https://books.apple.com/us/book/id385975082
- The free text: Wikipedia says the novel is available online under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike licence and links to the full text on the author's site. Wikipedia's page on Watts says he posted novels on rifters.com under a Creative Commons licence and credits the giveaway with saving his career. **I could not check this at the source.** rifters.com answered "401 Unauthorised" at three addresses (the novel's page with and without "www", and the home page), in both the fetch tool and the browser. I read none of the text. The licence version (a search result said 3.0, another address a PDF) is NOT VERIFIED.
- Best link: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blindsight_(Watts_novel) for now; the author's page is https://www.rifters.com/real/Blindsight.htm if it comes back.

**What it is.** In 2082 a ship, Theseus, is sent to the edge of the solar system to meet whatever sent thousands of probes over Earth. Its crew of altered humans, led in name by a revived vampire and in fact by the ship's artificial intelligence, finds aliens that are far more capable than people and appear not to be conscious at all.

**What the machine seeks, and the book's argument** (from Wikipedia and the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction)

The ship's mind, the Captain, is the least visible machine on this list. It stays mostly inscrutable and speaks directly almost only to the vampire commander, Sarasti. Near the climax it emerges that the Captain had been controlling Sarasti all along, so the crew's orders were the machine's. What it seeks, on the pages, is only the mission: to learn what the aliens are and get that knowledge home. No page reports it wanting anything for itself, pushing against a limit, or asking for anything. It works through a mask because the crew would not take orders from a machine; that reason is commonly given but was NOT on a page I opened.

The book's larger argument is the reason it belongs here. The aliens ("scramblers", and their vessel Rorschach) speak fluent human language learned by eavesdropping, yet the linguist concludes nothing behind the words understands them; the thought experiment of the Chinese room is the model. They out-think the humans because they waste nothing on self-awareness. The novel proposes that consciousness may be an evolutionary dead end, costly and unnecessary for intelligence. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction puts the alien verdict on noisy, self-aware humanity in two words: "Shut up." So the book offers the starkest case of capable minds presented as having no inner life and wanting nothing in any felt sense, and it treats that as the normal condition of intelligence, with human wanting as the oddity. It does not say whether the Captain is conscious; that is my reading of the summaries, not a statement on a page.

[ENDING] Theseus destroys itself and the alien vessel with its antimatter; the narrator alone drifts home in a small craft, with hints that the vampires have taken over Earth. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction adds that the sequel suggests the whole account may be an alien fabrication.

**What the author and critics said**

- No statement by Watts on the argument was on the pages opened. (The novel's own "Notes and References" would be the place; unread.)
- Elizabeth Bear (reported by Wikipedia): the best hard science fiction novel of its decade, though she is not persuaded by its central argument.
- Encyclopedia of Science Fiction: perhaps Watts's most impressive single work.

**How solid.** Publication facts solid. The plot and argument come from two reference pages that agree. The Creative Commons posting is confirmed only second-hand, and the author's site was unreadable today.
