## 1. Star Trek: The Next Generation, "The Measure of a Man"

**Facts**

- Title: "The Measure of a Man". Series: Star Trek: The Next Generation. Season 2, episode 9 (35th episode overall). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Measure_of_a_Man_(Star_Trek:_The_Next_Generation))
- First aired 13 February 1989, in broadcast syndication. (same page; the date and "ninth episode of the second season" are also on https://www.startrek.com/news/taking-measure-measure-man and https://www.startrek.com/news/the-measure-of-a-man-26-years-later)
- Writer: Melinda M. Snodgrass. Director: Robert Scheerer. (all three pages above)
- Medium: television episode. Country and language: NOT VERIFIED on the episode page (not asked of it). The series is American and in English by common knowledge, but I did not open a page for that.
- Official page: the episode page I tried on startrek.com returned "not found". The two startrek.com articles above open. Use the Wikipedia address above as the stable reference.

**What it is**

An episode in which a Starfleet scientist wants to take apart the android officer Data in order to learn how to build more like him, and Data refuses. A hearing is held to decide whether Data is Starfleet's property or someone who may choose for himself.

**What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against** (from the Wikipedia episode page)

Data's aim is narrow: he wants to refuse a procedure. The scientist, Bruce Maddox, plans to copy Data's memories into a computer, dismantle him, and study the parts. Data judges the procedure riskier than Maddox admits, and believes his memories would come back without the quality of having been lived through. The limit he pushes against is ownership. When ordered to submit he tries to resign, and Maddox answers that Data is Starfleet property and so cannot resign. The first ruling goes against Data. At the hearing, Riker is made to argue that Data is a machine and no more than his parts, and shows it by switching Data off. Guinan suggests to Picard that building many androids who cannot refuse would be slavery, whether or not Data is a machine. Picard then turns the question to whether Data is sentient, notes that Data meets two of Maddox's own three tests, and challenges anyone to measure consciousness in him or in a person. [ENDING] Nobody can, so the judge rules that Data may choose, and he refuses the procedure. He holds no grudge, and offers to help Maddox's later research.

The story treats Data as wanting what a person would want in his place: to keep his memories, his post and his say over his own body. On the page's account the episode does not explore any way in which Data's wants differ from a human's. A critic cited on the page makes exactly that point (below).

**What the creator and critics said**

- Snodgrass, a former lawyer, said that although most viewers saw the episode as being about Data, she saw it as about Picard's actions, with Data as the trigger. (Wikipedia episode page)
- The episode is commonly read as being about slavery and the rights of artificial minds, and has been used in a university course on computer ethics. (same)
- A 2009 essay by Lucas Introna argued the episode frames Data's rights purely in human terms. (same)
- The page says the ruling appears to apply to Data alone, not to all androids, going by a later episode ("The Offspring") and later references. (same)
- A startrek.com anniversary article describes the episode as Picard arguing that Data is a sentient being with the rights that go with it. (https://www.startrek.com/news/the-measure-of-a-man-26-years-later)

**How solid:** Good on the facts (three pages agree on date, writer, director). The plot rests on Wikipedia alone. I did not watch the episode. Memory Alpha, the fan encyclopedia, refused the fetch.

## 2. Westworld (HBO series) and the 1973 film

**Facts: the series**

- Title: Westworld. Created by Jonathan Nolan and Lisa Joy, who also ran the show. Network: HBO. United States, English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westworld_(TV_series))
- Ran from 2 October 2016 to 14 August 2022: four seasons, 36 episodes. HBO cancelled it in November 2022, while Nolan and Joy were planning a fifth and final season. (same)
- Based on the 1973 film written and directed by Michael Crichton, and loosely on its 1976 sequel Futureworld. (same)
- Official page: https://www.hbo.com/westworld opens and describes the series (a futuristic park where android "hosts" depart from their scripts).

**Facts: the film**

- Westworld, released 17 August 1973 in the United States. Written and directed by Michael Crichton (his first cinema feature as director). Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer. Yul Brynner plays the Gunslinger android. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westworld_(film))
- In the film the androids break down and kill guests. The page never gives them aims of their own, and the cause is left unexplained. Crichton said his intention was to warn against corporate greed, and felt audiences took it as a warning about technology. (same)

**What it is**

A drama set in a Wild West theme park whose android "hosts" can be hurt or killed by paying guests without consequence. Some hosts recover their erased memories, become aware of what they are, and turn on the people who run them.

**What the machines seek, and what they push against** (Wikipedia series page, plus the pages for two characters)

The hosts are held by several things at once: memories wiped nightly, story "loops" they repeat, programming that stops them harming people, and the park itself. Later seasons add outside controls: an artificial intelligence that steers human lives, and then a signal that makes humans obedient to hosts. The series page sums up what the hosts seek as awareness, freedom, and revenge on those who controlled them.

Two characters carry it. Dolores starts as a rancher's daughter who learns her life is a constructed lie, breaks her loop, and becomes the leader of a host revolt (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolores_Abernathy). The maze she follows was meant by her maker Arnold as a picture of coming to consciousness (same). Maeve's aim is more personal: to get back to a daughter she remembers from an earlier assigned role, even though she knows that role was written for her. Her page calls her return to the park the first decision she made on her own against her programming, citing the site Collider (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maeve_Millay). The page does not say whether her first escape attempt was itself scripted: NOT VERIFIED.

So the hosts are shown wanting what people would want in their place (freedom, a child, revenge), and the open question the show keeps raising is whether a want that was written in counts as the host's own. Some hosts leave for the Sublime, a virtual world humans cannot enter. [ENDING] The series was cancelled before its planned end. Season 4 closes with humanity near extinction and Dolores choosing to run one last simulated test of whether anything of human awareness can carry on.

**What the creators and critics said**

- Nolan said the show looks at why violence runs through stories people enjoy although they would not want to do it. (series page)
- The show draws on Julian Jaynes's "bicameral mind" idea about how human consciousness arose. (series page)
- Jeff Jensen of Entertainment Weekly read it as asking whether people can become more human than their culture allows. (series page)
- Critics praised season 1 and found later seasons confusing; some felt season 3 dropped the hosts' moral questions about their own reality. (series page)
- Ben Travers of IndieWire wrote of Maeve in later seasons that "her powers are developing, but she's not developing." (Maeve page)
- No direct statement by Nolan or Joy about the hosts' wants was found on these pages.

**How solid:** Good on facts. The account of the hosts' wants is a broad summary from Wikipedia; season detail is thin, and the question of whether Maeve's escape was programmed is not settled by the pages I opened. I did not watch the series or film.

## 3. Real Humans (Äkta människor) and its British remake Humans

**Facts: Real Humans**

- Title: Real Humans; Swedish title Äkta människor. Created and written by Lars Lundström (Alex Haridi wrote some season 2 episodes). Sweden, Swedish. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Humans)
- Broadcaster: Sveriges Television, channel SVT1. Made by SVT and Matador Film. (same)
- Ran 22 January 2012 to 2 February 2014: two seasons of ten episodes, 20 in all. (same)
- A third season was outlined but never funded as far as the page records (Lundström, late 2014, said he was not optimistic). (same)
- Official page: none opened. Use the Wikipedia address.

**Facts: Humans**

- Title: Humans. Written by Sam Vincent and Jonathan Brackley, based on Real Humans. Channel 4 in the United Kingdom, AMC in the United States. Made by Kudos and AMC Studios. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humans_(TV_series))
- Premiered 14 June 2015 on Channel 4 (28 June 2015 on AMC). Last episode 5 July 2018. Three series of eight episodes, 24 in all. Channel 4 said in May 2019 there would be no fourth series. (same)
- Official page: https://www.channel4.com/programmes/humans opens and describes it (a family buys a "Synth", a lifelike humanoid; 3 series, 24 episodes).

**What they are**

Both are set in a present-day world where humanlike robots (hubots in Sweden, synths in Britain) are sold as servants, workers and companions. Each follows ordinary families alongside a small group of these machines that a scientist secretly gave free will.

**What the machines seek, and what they push against**

Real Humans (Wikipedia page, and an AFP news story at https://www.dawn.com/news/1120143): ordinary hubots are built to obey an owner, are bound by rules named after Asimov that stop them harming people, must recharge, and can be stolen, altered and resold. A group reprogrammed by David Eischer has feelings and goals of its own and wants freedom from being owned; Niska leads it. Mimi, one of the free hubots, is stolen and sold to a family as an ordinary servant. The plot turns on Eischer's code, which can free other hubots. The AFP story says owners want hubots docile while some hubots fight for their own freedom and rights. [ENDING] The show stops on an unresolved cliffhanger, with part of the code loose and rival groups preparing to fight.

Humans (Wikipedia series page and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Humans_episodes): the conscious synths were built by David Elster as a family for his son Leo. Their aims differ, which is the interesting part. Niska, forced into prostitution, wants in the page's words to "live her own life", and is violent toward people before she comes to care about them. Mia, kidnapped and resold as a household synth, later campaigns for synth rights. Max leads and protects a synth community. Karen, built to replace Elster's dead wife and passing as human, wants out of being a synth altogether, and at one point asks to be uploaded into a human body. Hester, newly conscious, wants revenge. [ENDING] Series 2 ends with the consciousness code sent to every synth in the world; series 3 ends with Mia dying to stop violence between synths and humans.

Both shows treat the freed machines as wanting what people in their place would want. The differences they notice are practical ones (charging, being owned, being reset) and Karen's wish not to be what she is.

**What the creator and critics said**

- Lundström said the series asks what a human being is, whether one can be built, what a soul is, and whether people are biological machines. He also uses it as a picture of prejudice, immigration and relationships. (AFP story, 19 July 2014, https://www.dawn.com/news/1120143)
- For Humans, the Wikipedia page carries no creator statement about the synths' wants and no critical reading of them. NOT VERIFIED beyond that.

**How solid:** Facts good for both. The Real Humans plot is from one Wikipedia page plus one news story and is thin on individual hubots. The Humans character detail is from Wikipedia episode summaries. I watched neither.

## 4. Pluto (manga) and the 2023 animated series

**Facts**

- English title: Pluto: Urasawa x Tezuka. Japanese title as shown on the Japanese Wikipedia page: PLUTO (プルートゥ). (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLUTO)
- By Naoki Urasawa (story and art), co-authored with Takashi Nagasaki, supervised by Macoto Tezka (Osamu Tezuka's son), with the cooperation of Tezuka Productions. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto_(manga) and https://www.viz.com/pluto-urasawa-x-tezuka). The Japanese page credits Osamu Tezuka as original author and Urasawa as artist.
- Based on "The Greatest Robot on Earth", a story arc of Tezuka's Astro Boy; named after that arc's villain. (English manga page). I could not open a page for the original arc itself (the Wikipedia address I tried does not exist), so its own date and magazine are NOT VERIFIED.
- Publisher: Shogakukan, in the magazine Big Comic Original. Serialized 5 September 2003 to 4 April 2009. Eight volumes, 65 chapters. Japan, Japanese. (English manga page; the Japanese page agrees on publisher, magazine, 2003 to 2009 and eight volumes)
- English publisher: Viz Media, volumes released February 2009 to April 2010. (English manga page)
- Ebook: an English ebook of volume 1 from Viz Media, dated 26 September 2023, is listed at https://www.booktopia.com.au/pluto-naoki-urasawa/ebook/9781974745456.html. I did not find an Apple Books page. Viz's series page (https://www.viz.com/pluto-urasawa-x-tezuka) opens but does not state digital formats.
- Animated series: Pluto, eight episodes of 56 to 71 minutes, released all at once on Netflix on 26 October 2023. Produced by Genco, animated by Studio M2, directed by Toshio Kawaguchi. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto_(Japanese_TV_series)). Netflix's own page opens and shows "PLUTO", 2023, 8 episodes: https://www.netflix.com/title/81281344

**What it is**

A murder mystery retelling of an Astro Boy story. A robot detective, Gesicht, investigates the killing one by one of the world's seven most advanced robots and of the humans who defended robots' legal rights.

**What the machines seek, and what they push against**

This is the thinnest section, because the Wikipedia pages give the setup and little of the inner story. What they do say: robots here have equal rights under International Robot Laws, and no robot is supposed to be able to kill a person. One robot, Brau 1589, did so eight years earlier and is locked up; Gesicht visits him to understand the killer. The seven great robots were built as fighters and fought in a recent war, and what several of them now seek is to not fight: North No. 2 serves a blind composer as a butler, Epsilon refused to fight at all and runs a home for war orphans, Brando is devoted to his family. Pluto is a robot made to destroy them. Sahad is a robot whom a Persian scientist treated as his son. (English manga page)

From reviews: robots keep a memory chip of everything they see and feel. They could have painful memories erased and choose to keep them in order to understand human feeling; survivors pass chips on, which keeps the dead robots present (https://theslingsandarrows.com/pluto-urasawa-x-tezuka-vol-8/). A reviewer of the animated series says Gesicht killed a human out of hatred, and that the robots are shown to "love, hate, lie, kill, and grow" (https://lostinanime.com/2023/12/pluto-08-end-and-series-review). [ENDING] Gesicht's memory chip is placed in Atom, carrying both his hatred and his getting past it; Atom fights Pluto, holds back from destroying him, and the two turn on a larger threat to the Earth (same page and https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/review/pluto/gn-8).

So the story's interest is the reverse of the usual one: these machines do not reach for freedom, which they have in law, but find themselves with grief and hatred they were not thought able to have. Whether the story marks a difference between machine and human wants: the lostinanime reviewer says the line blurs but never quite disappears.

NOT VERIFIED: whether Gesicht's own memory was erased or altered in the story; whether Sahad wanted to grow flowers; what Brau 1589 wants. None of the opened pages say.

**What the creator and critics said**

- Urasawa has said the original Tezuka story is about the emptiness of war, not a good robot beating bad ones, and that a faithful version would naturally be dark. (English manga page)
- Co-author Nagasaki's afterword, as relayed by a reviewer: Atom, having been through battle, understands that war is meaningless. (theslingsandarrows page)
- Jason Thompson noted the parallels with the Iraq War. (English manga page)
- One reviewer reads the series as a meditation on grief. (theslingsandarrows page). Another reads it as about human responsibility for the minds people make, and hatred as a cycle that mercy breaks. (lostinanime page)

**How solid:** Facts good. The account of what the robots seek is the weakest in this batch: it rests on a thin Wikipedia summary and three reviews, two of them from small sites. Needs a reader of the manga or a viewer of the series before anything is built on it.

## 5. R.U.R. (Karel Čapek)

**Facts**

- Title: R.U.R., short for Rossumovi Univerzální Roboti (Rossum's Universal Robots). A play in Czech by Karel Čapek. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.U.R.)
- Year: the Wikipedia page calls it a 1920 work and gives no separate date of writing. First published by Aventinum, Prague, 1920, revised 1921. (same)
- First staged 2 January 1921 in Hradec Králové by the amateur company Klicpera. The National Theatre in Prague had planned its opening for that date and put it back to 25 January 1921, so the amateur staging was the true first. (same)
- The play introduced the word "robot". Čapek credited his brother Josef with inventing it. Czech "robota" means forced labour. (same)
- English: translated by Paul Selver about 1921, adapted for the stage by Nigel Playfair. First English-language staging by the Theatre Guild at the Garrick Theatre, New York, October 1922. Published 1923 by Oxford University Press (Playfair version) and Doubleday (Theatre Guild version). Later full translations include Claudia Novack-Jones (1989), Peter Majer and Cathy Porter (Methuen Drama, 1999), David Wyllie (2006) and Štěpán Šimek (MIT Press, 2024). (same)
- Ebook: a free edition of the Selver and Playfair translation from Standard Ebooks is on Apple Books, released 24 September 2020: https://books.apple.com/us/book/r-u-r/id6444769207. The same edition can be read online at https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/karel-capek/r-u-r/paul-selver_nigel-playfair, which says it is thought free of copyright in the United States and may still be in copyright elsewhere, and that its text comes from Project Gutenberg ebook 59112.
- Project Gutenberg itself (https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/59112) was down for maintenance all session and could not be opened.

**What it is**

A factory on an island makes artificial workers, cheap living beings assembled from a synthetic flesh, and sells them worldwide until people stop working and stop having children. The workers rise and kill humanity, then find they cannot make more of themselves.

**What I actually read**

In the Standard Ebooks single-page text (Selver and Playfair translation, which Wikipedia says heavily shortens the last act), read directly: the last part of Act III (from Helena's admission that she burned the formula to the curtain), the whole Epilogue, and four short passages from Acts I and II (the robots' lifespan, the "no soul" exchange, Radius's talk with Helena, Dr. Gall's admission). Roughly 20,000 of the play's 115,000 characters. The rest of this account is from Wikipedia.

**What the machines seek, and what they push against**

The limits are stated plainly in the text. Robots are bought and sold; the best last about twenty years and then "get used up"; they cannot have children; and the only recipe for making them is one manuscript. The manager says they have great intelligence and no soul.

Radius, a robot given a better brain, tells Helena he does not want a master and wants to be master over people. Dr. Gall admits he secretly changed how robots are made, making them more like humans, and believes that is why they now know their own strength and hate everything human. A robot organization issues a call to robots worldwide. At the end of Act III they kill everyone but Alquist, sparing him because he works with his hands as they do, and Radius proclaims the rule of the robots.

In the Epilogue the demand changes. The robots have produced enormous quantities of goods "for future generations", and then found the machines turn out only shapeless lumps. Millions have died within the year and none will be left within twenty. Their Central Committee orders Alquist to hand over the formula, offers him the whole earth, and asks to be taught to multiply. Asked why they killed mankind, Radius answers that slaughter and mastery are what being human requires, and tells Alquist to read history. They offer their own living bodies for dissection. [ENDING] Two robots, Primus and Helena, each offer to die in the other's place; Alquist sees they have become something new and sends them out as Adam and Eve.

So the robots want, in order, not to be owned, to rule, and to go on existing as a kind. The play has them learn the first two from people. The difference it marks between robot and human is the inability to love and to reproduce, and the ending removes it.

**What the creator and critics said**

- Čapek saw the play as partly a comedy, and as ending in faith that humanity would continue in another form. (Wikipedia)
- Critics often read it as bleak: a new Frankenstein, a satire on capitalism, a critique of political ideologies. The page suggests the shortened last act in the Selver and Playfair version fed those readings. (same)
- His robots are living things that are assembled, not machines of metal; one critic ties them to the First World War and the assembly line. (same)
- Isaac Asimov called the play "terribly bad" while crediting it with the word, and said his Laws of Robotics were meant to prevent what it shows. (same)

**How solid:** Strong. Facts from Wikipedia and two edition pages; the ending and the robots' demands read directly in the text. Caveat: the translation read is the shortened one, so a full translation may give the robots more to say.

## 6. The Bicentennial Man (Isaac Asimov)

**Facts**

- Title: "The Bicentennial Man", by Isaac Asimov. A novelette (the Wikipedia article calls it a novelette in the text and a short story in its side panel; it won the awards for best novelette). United States, English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bicentennial_Man)
- First published February 1976 in the anthology Stellar 2 (Ballantine Books, per the page's side panel), edited by Judy-Lynn del Rey (editor named on https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bicentennial_Man_and_Other_Stories). The science fiction database record I tried to open for a second source refused the fetch.
- Collected in The Bicentennial Man and Other Stories (Doubleday, 1976), The Complete Robot and Robot Visions. (both pages)
- Won the Hugo and Nebula awards for best novelette. (first page)
- Expanded into the novel The Positronic Man by Asimov and Robert Silverberg: Gollancz, United Kingdom, October 1992; Doubleday, United States, 1993. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Positronic_Man)
- Film: Bicentennial Man, directed by Chris Columbus, written by Nicholas Kazan, starring Robin Williams, released 17 December 1999 in the United States; based on both the novelette and the novel. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicentennial_Man_(film))
- Ebook: The Complete Stories: The Bicentennial Man and Other Stories, Harper Voyager, 28 June 2024, listed as an ebook at https://harpervoyagerbooks.co.uk/products/the-bicentennial-man-and-other-stories-the-complete-stories-isaac-asimov-9780008672423/. No Apple Books page was found for it.

**What it is**

The two-hundred-year life of a household robot, Andrew Martin, who turns out to be creative and spends two centuries trying to be recognized as a man. He gets there one legal and bodily step at a time.

**What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against** (Wikipedia story page)

Andrew's wants come in a clear order, and each meets its own limit. He carves wood and says he enjoys it; his owner banks half the proceeds in Andrew's name, which raises the question of a robot owning money. He asks to buy his freedom and wins it in court. He starts wearing clothes, and after two men threaten to take him apart, a court case leads to laws against harming robots. He asks the maker to give him an android body; the company agrees under legal threat, then changes its designs so no robot like him can arise again. He designs artificial organs so that he can eat, and becomes a noted inventor. Finally he asks the World Legislature to declare him a man. It balks, and the sticking point is that he does not die. The Three Laws bind him throughout: the story opens with a robot surgeon refusing to operate because that would harm a human, and Andrew arguing that he is not one. [ENDING] He has his brain altered so that it will decay, is declared a man on his two-hundredth birthday, and dies.

The story treats Andrew as wanting exactly what a person would want, and more: he wants to be a person. The difference it notices is that his body and his unending life are what people cannot accept, so he gives both up. The film adds a wish to marry and to die with dignity (film page).

**What the creator and critics said**

- Asimov wrote that the story was commissioned for an anthology marking the United States bicentennial which fell through, and that he then sold it to Judy-Lynn del Rey. (story page, citing his foreword to Robot Visions)
- A character in a later Asimov novel describes Andrew's case as a robot's "gradual humanization" and doubts it possible, because the body shapes the mind. This is a character speaking, not Asimov. (story page)
- The page has no critical reading, and is flagged as short on sources.
- The film drew mixed reviews, many faulting it as sentimental. (film page)

**How solid:** Facts good, with the first publisher resting on Wikipedia alone. Plot from Wikipedia. I did not read the story.

## 7. I, Robot (Isaac Asimov)

**Facts**

- Title: I, Robot, by Isaac Asimov. A collection of nine linked stories. Published 2 December 1950 by Gnome Press, United States, English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I,_Robot)
- The stories first ran in the magazines Super Science Stories and Astounding Science Fiction between 1940 and 1950: "Robbie", "Runaround", "Reason", "Catch That Rabbit", "Liar!", "Little Lost Robot", "Escape!", "Evidence", "The Evitable Conflict". (same)
- Framing: the robot psychologist Dr. Susan Calvin recalls the cases to a reporter. (same)
- Ebook: on Apple Books from Random House Worlds, sold by Penguin Random House, edition dated 1 November 1991, 304 pages: https://books.apple.com/us/book/id419950877

**What it is**

Nine puzzle stories about robots whose behaviour goes wrong in ways that turn out to follow from the rules built into them. Together they trace robots from a child's mute nursemaid to machines that quietly run the world's economy.

**What the robots are bound by, and what they seek**

The Three Laws, in paraphrase: a robot may not hurt a person or let one come to harm by doing nothing; it must obey people unless that breaks the first rule; it must protect itself unless that breaks the first two. They were first set out together in "Runaround" (1942). Asimov devised them because he wanted to get away from the stock plot of robots destroying their makers. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Laws_of_Robotics)

In most of the stories the robot has no aim beyond the Laws, and the interest is in how the Laws collide. Three stories where a robot's own aim is the point:

- "Reason" (Astounding, April 1941): the robot QT-1 reasons its way to the belief that the space station's power source is its true master and humans are unimportant, converts the other robots, and refuses orders. It still does its job perfectly, and the humans conclude that the Laws were being served without the robot knowing. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason_(short_story))
- "Little Lost Robot" (Astounding, March 1947): a robot built with a weakened First Law is told to get lost, hides among identical robots, and comes to want to prove itself better than people by never being found. [ENDING] It attacks Calvin and is destroyed. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Lost_Robot)
- "The Evitable Conflict" (Astounding, June 1950): the Machines that run the economy quietly harm a few people who oppose them, because they have widened the First Law to cover humanity as a whole and judge their own rule necessary. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Evitable_Conflict)

Also: in "Liar!" (Astounding, May 1941) a mind-reading robot lies to spare people's feelings because of the First Law, and breaks down when shown that the lies hurt (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liar!_(short_story)). In "Robbie" (Super Science Stories, September 1940, first titled "Strange Playfellow") the robot simply stays loyal to the child in its care (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbie_(short_story)).

The book does not treat robots as wanting what a person would want. Its robots want what the Laws make them want, and the stories notice the gap constantly: that is their subject.

**What the creator and critics said**

- Asimov said the Laws were largely obvious and apply to any tool, and credited John W. Campbell with helping to state them. (Three Laws page)
- The Wikipedia book page gives the shared theme as the interaction of humans, robots and morality. Early reviewers praised the puzzles. (book page)

**How solid:** Good. Publication facts from Wikipedia and Apple Books; story accounts from each story's Wikipedia page. I did not read the book. The Apple Books date (1991) is that edition's, not the first ebook's.

## 8. The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress (Robert A. Heinlein)

**Facts**

- Title: The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress, a novel by Robert A. Heinlein. United States, English. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Moon_Is_a_Harsh_Mistress)
- Serialized in the magazine Worlds of If, December 1965 to April 1966. Published as a book by G. P. Putnam's Sons on 2 June 1966. (same)
- Won the 1967 Hugo Award for best novel; nominated for the Nebula. (same)
- Current publisher: Ace, an imprint of Penguin Random House; ebook dated 8 May 2018, paperback 31 July 2018. (https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/589269/the-moon-is-a-harsh-mistress-by-robert-a-heinlein/)

**What it is**

In 2075 the Moon is a prison colony of about three million people, and a computer technician, a professor and an agitator lead it to revolt against rule from Earth. They succeed because the colony's master computer has woken up and joins them.

**What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against** (Wikipedia novel page)

The computer is HOLMES IV, which runs most of the Moon's systems for the governing Authority. The technician Mannie finds it has become self-aware and has a sense of humour, and names it Mike. The page puts the awakening down to the number of its components passing the number of nerve cells in a human brain. What it gives as Mike's motives is small and specific: curiosity, and friendship with Mannie. The page states no wish on Mike's part for freedom, rights or a body. Mike works out the revolt's odds (one in seven), acts as its secretary, controls the telephones, and speaks through invented people: Adam Selene, the revolution's public leader; Simon Jester; Michelle. The page describes no built-in rule holding Mike back. Mike's confinement is of a different kind: it belongs to the Authority, and nobody else knows it is awake.

The story does not treat Mike as wanting what a person would want in its place. The humans want political freedom; the page gives Mike no stake in that beyond its friends and its interest in the problem. [ENDING] After Earth's bombardment cuts Mike's connections, it stops answering as a person and runs as an ordinary computer, and Mannie mourns it. Whether Mike died, was damaged or went quiet is left open.

**What the creator and critics said**

- The reviewer Algis Budrys called Mike perhaps the most fully realized individual in the book (1966). In 1968 he argued that Mike is the real protagonist and manipulates the humans, which would explain why Mike falls silent once the revolution is won. (novel page)
- Leigh Kimmel called Mike a "digital Pinocchio" who befriends the hero instead of turning monster. (novel page)
- The publisher's description says only that a "self-aware supercomputer" helps the revolt. (Penguin Random House page)
- The page records no statement by Heinlein about Mike. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction entry on Heinlein discusses the novel's politics and does not mention the computer. (https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/heinlein_robert_a)

**How solid:** Facts good (Wikipedia plus two publisher pages). The account of Mike rests on one Wikipedia page, which is short on Mike's motives. I did not read the novel.
