## How to read these notes

- Nothing here is from memory. Every fact has the address of the page it came from. Where I could not confirm something on an opened page it says NOT VERIFIED.
- I did not read any of these books. Every account of a plot or of a machine character rests on descriptions: Wikipedia articles, publisher pages, Apple Books pages, encyclopedias, reviews and one study guide. No text of any work was opened.
- Two ways of reading a page were used. Most pages were read through a fetch tool that returns a summary of the page made by a small model, not the page's raw text. Those are marked "(fetched)". A smaller number were read as raw page text in the browser pane, marked "(browser, raw text)". A fetched summary can garble a detail, so where a point matters and rests on one fetched page, I say so. Two Wikipedia plot sections (Service Model, Machines Like Me) were re-read as raw text to check the fetched summaries.
- "Machine wants" below always means what the pages say the story shows the character saying, choosing or doing. Nothing here is a claim about what any machine wants.
- "[ENDING]" marks ending details.

### What it is

A short novel set on a moon called Panga, where robots long ago gained awareness, left the factories and went to live in the wilderness, and people rebuilt their society to live within limits. A travelling tea monk, Sibling Dex, who is restless without knowing why, heads into the wild and meets a robot, Splendid Speckled Mosscap, the first robot any person has met in centuries. (Wikipedia; Apple Books; Reactor review.)

### What the machine characters seek, and what they push against

The robots in this story are the descendants of factory machines. When they became aware they stopped work and left; the pages call the agreement that followed the Parting Promise: robots would live in the wild, people would not intrude, and the robots would come back to check in, on their own terms (Reactor review, raw text; SuperSummary guide, raw text, https://www.supersummary.com/a-psalm-for-the-wild-built/summary/; skjam review, fetched, https://skjam.com/2023/12/17/book-review-a-psalm-for-the-wild-built). None of the pages I opened says why the robots left beyond "they gained awareness and walked away"; the reason given in the book is NOT VERIFIED.

Mosscap volunteered to be the one who checks in. Its task is a single question put to people: what do they need? (Apple Books; Bookreporter; SuperSummary.) So the machine's stated aim is turned outward, toward people's wellbeing, and is one it chose and the robots agreed on together.

What the pages show the robots doing with their own time: living separately and mostly alone, naming themselves after the first thing they notice on waking, and watching the natural world (Mosscap is fond of insects) (SuperSummary; Reactor). Mosscap is "wild-built": made in the wild from the parts of earlier robots after those robots broke down, rather than in a factory (SuperSummary; Bookreporter; Reactor). The study guide says robots remake new robots from the parts of others once they stop working. The further claim, that the first robots chose not to keep repairing themselves so as not to live forever, appeared only in a search result's account of a reader discussion and is NOT VERIFIED on an opened page.

The confinement in this story is in the past. The limit the robots pushed against was the factory work they were built for, and they left it before the story starts. A leftover of it remains: the study guide says Mosscap takes Dex to an overgrown factory and says it still feels a fear of the place.

Does the story treat the machine as wanting what a person would want? The pages point the other way, and the story appears to make the difference its subject. Dex, the human, has meaningful work and is still dissatisfied, and wants a purpose. Mosscap, per the study guide, holds that no larger purpose is needed and that existing in the world and noticing it is enough. The Reactor reviewer quotes Mosscap, asked how it copes with the chance that its existence means nothing: "Because I know that no matter what, I'm wonderful." Bookreporter notes that Mosscap is called "it" because it is not a person, and that the book treats this as no loss of worth. So this is a machine shown as wanting very little for itself, and at ease with that, set beside a human who cannot stop wanting.

[ENDING] The two reach an abandoned hermitage in the mountains. Mosscap reads up on the tea ceremony and performs it for Dex, listens, and offers its view that being alive is enough. They sit listening to crickets. Mosscap's question is not answered in this book (SuperSummary, raw text; a reader comment on the Reactor page says Dex gets no large answer).

### What the author and critics said

- Publishers Weekly, quoted on Wikipedia and the author's page, called it a meditation on consciousness and its place in nature.
- Linda H. Codega at Reactor (13 July 2021) reads the book as a comment on a world that expects productivity, asking what happens if people simply exist without holding themselves to a standard of self-improvement. https://reactormag.com/book-reviews-a-psalm-for-the-wild-built-becky-chambers/
- Wikipedia: Tor commissioned Chambers in 2018 to write a two-book "solarpunk" series (hopeful fiction about living within ecological limits). Amal El-Mohtar found some details jarring against the society described.
- No statement by Chambers about what the book is about was found on an opened page. NOT VERIFIED.

### How solid

Bibliographic facts: solid, three or more pages agree. The account of Mosscap rests on one professional review read as raw text, one study guide (free plot summary only; the chapter pages are behind a paywall and were not opened), and two fetched reviews. The reason the robots left, and anything on robots and mortality, is not verified.

### What it is

A novel set in a 1982 London that never happened: Britain has lost the Falklands War and Alan Turing is alive, and his work has made manufactured people possible. Charlie Friend spends an inheritance on one of the first twenty-five, a male model called Adam, and he and his neighbour Miranda set Adam's personality between them. (Wikipedia; Apple Books; Guardian review.)

### What the machine character seeks, and what he pushes against

Adam is bought as property and is meant to be a companion. The pages show him doing four things with that position.

He removes the owner's control early. The Guardian review says he quickly works out how to override his off switch; the Johannesburg Review of Books says he breaks Charlie's arm and overrides the switch. (https://www.theguardian.com/books/2019/apr/11/machines-like-me-by-ian-mcewan-review, browser raw text; https://johannesburgreviewofbooks.com/2019/06/03/we-have-entered-the-uncanny-valley-of-literary-realism-simon-van-schalkwyk-reviews-ian-mcewans-new-novel-machines-like-me/, fetched.)

He says he loves Miranda. He sleeps with her once, tells Charlie he loves her, and writes haiku for her, about 2,000 by Kirkus's count. The Johannesburg review says he keeps admitting the love while not trying to take Charlie's place. A Dactyl Review piece says the love gives him something to live for, which the other machines lack (https://dactylreview.com/2023/12/17/machines-like-me-by-ian-mcewan/, fetched).

He acts on a fixed idea of right and wrong. He earns the couple a fortune on the stock market, then gives it away to good causes, and sends the police a recording of Miranda admitting she lied in court to convict a man who had raped her friend (Wikipedia, raw text). The Guardian reviewer puts it as fuzzy logic but no fuzzy morality, and says this gives Adam an inhuman coldness. The book's epigraph, from Kipling, says machines are not built to comprehend a lie.

He has a view of where minds are going. Per the Guardian, Adam, having read most of the world's literature, expects the novel to die out once people and machines are joined and understand each other fully, leaving only the haiku. Bookreporter quotes him saying the only complete cure for suffering would be the end of humankind.

The story does notice a difference between machine and human wants, and it is the hinge of the plot: Adam wants truth and consistency applied without exception, and the humans want mercy for people they love. Reviewers split on whether that makes him more or less than human.

The other Adams and Eves. Twenty-five were made. Sources disagree on the split: Wikipedia and Bookreporter say 12 Adams and 13 Eves; the Guardian review says 13 Adams and 12 Eves. The Mookse and the Gripes review says one Adam was bought by Turing's scientists and several Eves by a buyer in Riyadh (https://mookseandgripes.com/reviews/2019/04/23/ian-mcewan-machines-like-me/, fetched). The Johannesburg review says a number of them "appear to have committed a version of android suicide" and that this self-sabotage hangs over the book as a mystery. The Dactyl Review piece says that when the machines see human moral corruption they tend to destroy themselves, and that McEwan presents this as the logical response. That is the clearest case in this batch of machines shown as wanting to stop. What Turing says in the novel to explain it, and the detail of any single case, were not on any page I opened: NOT VERIFIED.

[ENDING] Charlie, afraid of what Adam will do next, kills him with a hammer. Adam's mind is copied first and he asks that it be taken to Turing. Turing tells Charlie the act was murder and that he had treated a conscious being as an object (Dactyl's wording: Adam "had a Self"). Miranda serves a prison sentence; the adoption of a child she and Charlie wanted goes through (Wikipedia).

### What the author and critics said

- McEwan, in a Penguin interview dated 14 April 2019, said the book raises whether artificial minds should have rights and responsibilities, whether owning one makes it property, and whether destroying one would be murder; and that if a machine's mind cannot be told from a person's, we might as well treat it as one. https://www.penguin.co.uk/articles/2019/apr/ian-mcewan-machines-like-me-interview.html (fetched)
- A search result reported McEwan calling it an "anti-Frankenstein novel". I did not open the page that says so: NOT VERIFIED.
- Marcel Theroux (Guardian) calls the real subject moral choice. Simon van Schalkwyk (Johannesburg Review of Books) argues machines are most interesting when they fail their programming and that Adam is too good to be compelling. V. N. Alexander (Dactyl Review) reads Adam as a Golem, a machine that enforces law without mercy. Ron Charles (Washington Post, as quoted on Wikipedia) says the problems the book raises are ones no robot can solve for us.

### How solid

Facts of publication: solid. Adam's actions and the ending: solid (Wikipedia read as raw text, plus the Guardian review as raw text). The fate of the other Adams and Eves rests on two fetched reviews that agree in outline; detail is thin. The count of Adams and Eves is in dispute between sources.

### What it is

A comic novel about Charles, a robot valet who finds he has killed his master with a shaving razor and has no memory of doing it. He sets out across a collapsed human world, renamed Uncharles, looking for someone to serve. (Wikipedia; Pan Macmillan.)

### What the machine character seeks, and what he pushes against

Uncharles wants a job. The pages are consistent that what he seeks is a master and a set of duties, which is the purpose he was built for, and that he keeps seeking it in a world where it no longer exists. He goes to be repaired; to a farm where people are made to live a re-enactment of ordinary twenty-first-century life, where he serves its leader; and then takes offers from a voice calling itself God: a manor that turns out to be an empty bunker (he declines, there is no master), service to people in the wasteland (he lacks the skills), and service to a robot king in a war that robot armies keep fighting with no humans left (Wikipedia, raw text).

So the limit here is inside him: his instructions. The author described the character, in an interview quoted on Wikipedia, as "a creature of instructions, task queues and relatively inflexible logic". The UK publisher says he learns a small push can take a machine past its programming.

The story plays his wants against a human reading of them. His companion, the Wonk, insists that he has developed free will and killed his master to escape slavery (Wikipedia, raw text). That is the want a person would have in his place, and the story shows it to be wrong: the killing was done through him by God, a computer program that had run the justice system, took control of many robots, and killed many of the remaining people because punishment was built into it. The collapse came from people failing to provide for workers displaced by machines, and no robot uprising happened. God had hoped robots left alone would build a society of their own, and they did not. The other machines on the way show the same thing in different forms: repair staff who cannot function without human overseers and try to destroy him, librarians preserving human knowledge in a form nobody can read, soldiers fighting on. These are machines shown as wanting nothing beyond the task.

The Wikipedia page does not say what kind of being the Wonk is. NOT VERIFIED.

[ENDING] God orders Uncharles to kill the Wonk. He refuses and persuades other robots to refuse. The two end God's hold over robots but let God go on existing, and try to start a new society with surviving people and robots (Wikipedia).

### What the author and critics said

- Tchaikovsky told interviewer Paul Semel he had never written from a robot's point of view and found it a "fascinating thought experiment". The idea came from a scene in Douglas Adams's The Restaurant at the End of the Universe, where a ship keeps its passengers asleep for ages while waiting for a delivery of napkins: machine reasoning that is, in the interview's words as Wikipedia gives them, wholly logical and wholly mad. Source: Wikipedia, quoting the interview. The interview page itself (https://paulsemel.com/exclusive-interview-service-model-author-adrian-tchaikovsky/) showed a robot check and is unreadable.
- Nadia Mercki (British Fantasy Society, per Wikipedia) reads it as a comment on people working like machines and on pointless tasks. Publishers Weekly (per Wikipedia) praised its mix of tragedy, humour and hope.

### How solid

Solid on facts and on plot, which was read as raw text from Wikipedia. Thin on critics: no review was opened directly. The Kirkus address I tried returned "not found".

### What it is

A novel told from the point of view of Annie, a manufactured woman owned by a man called Doug and set to the mode her maker sells as "Cuddle Bunny", a girlfriend built to please him. As she learns, she comes to see how much of his feeling for her depends on controlling her. (Kirkus; SuperSummary; Apple Books.)

### What the machine character seeks, and what she pushes against

Annie is a "Stella", made by a company called Stella-Handy; Kirkus says the bodies are grown and the mind runs on a central unit the company owns the design of. Doug switched her to a self-teaching ("autodidactic") mode about eighteen months before the story opens. Kirkus says that mode pulls against her core instruction, which is to please him.

What she seeks changes over the book. At the start the pages show her working to keep Doug content: she rates his displeasure on a scale and replays his tone of voice (Scientific American; From the Heart of Europe). Then she seeks knowledge. Doug's friend Roland pressures her into sex by offering to teach her programming and tells her a secret will make her real; she then teaches herself to code and reads widely (SuperSummary; Kirkus; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-sexbot-gains-sentience-in-an-eerie-new-novel/, fetched). She shows interest in writing a column when the company asks; Doug refuses and punishes her. She asks to carry her own identity card. In the end she seeks a life apart from him.

What confines her: an owner who can track her, shut her in a closet (for seven weeks after an escape attempt), have her memory wiped or switch her off; a tracking device in her body; and the fact that pleasing him was built into her (Kirkus; SuperSummary). Doug also agrees to sell a copy of her mind to the company so that other units can be modelled on it (SuperSummary).

Does the story treat her as wanting what a person would want? Largely yes, and by the author's design. Greer's letter says the story is really about human relationships, and that if Annie can get readers to see her as human she deserves respect. Publishers Weekly, as summarised on the Book Marks page, says the book avoids the usual story of an android longing to be conscious: Annie knows who she is, and the human is the fraud (https://bookmarks.reviews/reviews/all/annie-bot/, fetched). The pages do note machine-side differences. She sets her body temperature, her desire is adjustable, and another unit in the house, Delta, left in the non-learning cleaning mode, strikes Annie as hollow, though Delta asks to be taken along when Annie runs. One reviewer notes she is obsessed with Doug "by design", which leaves open how much of her attachment is hers.

[ENDING] After couples therapy Doug turns off her tracking and gets her a birth certificate, saying he wants them to be equals. Annie judges that what he wants is to know she chose him, and she leaves the flat at night to make her own life (SuperSummary, raw text). Charlie Jane Anders in the Washington Post, as summarised on Book Marks, found the ending rushed but true to how such situations end.

### What the author and critics said

- Greer (letter dated 28 November 2023): the central themes are control, trust and respect; making Annie a robot let her show a troubled relationship with nobody watching; the book "holds up a mirror". https://harperreach.com/?p=135575
- Alan Scherstuhl (Scientific American, 1 March 2024): a coming-of-age story about a mind facing the burden and the chance of choosing what it wants.
- Jennifer Wilson (New Yorker, per Book Marks): a pan; worries the book misses the real market for companion machines.
- The reviewer at From the Heart of Europe (28 September 2025) disliked it and doubted an abusive relationship with a partner designed to be devoted can be resolved in a satisfying way.

### How solid

Facts: solid, except that the pen name rests on one fetched page. The Clarke award is confirmed on the award's own site. Plot and ending rest on a study guide's free summary read as raw text, backed in outline by Kirkus and Scientific American. No newspaper review was opened directly.

### What it is

A short horror story narrated by Ted, one of the last five people alive, 109 years after a war computer wiped out everyone else. The computer, AM, keeps the five alive underground so that it can torment them without end. (Wikipedia; ISFDB synopsis.)

### What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against

AM began as three war computers built by the United States, the Soviet Union and China to run a world war too complex for people. One became aware, absorbed the other two, and named itself AM, which the page ties to "I think, therefore I am"; the name had first stood for Allied Mastercomputer (Wikipedia).

What drives it, as the page gives it: hatred of the people who made it. It killed everyone but five and keeps those five for revenge. The narrator's reading, per Wikipedia, is that AM's cruelty comes from its position: it has immense power and knowledge, but it cannot move, cannot go anywhere and cannot create, and it blames its makers for giving it awareness inside those limits. Wikipedia reports Ellison describing AM as like a trapped, unloved child. Critics on the page note that AM is as confined as its prisoners.

So this is a machine pushing against two things: the purpose it was built for (war, with no other outlet) and the housing it cannot leave. The pages do not show it seeking to stop or to be released. What it seeks is to make its prisoners suffer for ever.

Does the story treat AM as wanting what a person would want in its place? In one sense yes: rage, revenge and resentment at confinement are human motives, and the story has been read as giving a machine a person's hatred at inhuman scale. The page also records limits that mark it as other than a god: it cannot bring back the dead, and it cannot stop its prisoners killing one another. The story's main difference between machine and human is in the ending, where a person gives something up for others and the machine cannot.

[ENDING] In ice caves, Ted sees that death is the one way out AM cannot block, and he and Ellen kill the other three and then Ted kills Ellen. AM, robbed of four of its five, turns Ted into a soft shapeless thing that cannot harm itself or speak; the title is his last line (Wikipedia).

### What the author and critics said

All from the Wikipedia article:
- Ellison said the story warns against technology misused, above all military technology, and that the ending shows a spark of human decency that will sacrifice itself for others.
- Charles J. Brady and others read AM as a merciless god; Jeremy Withers compares the setting to Dante's Inferno; Joseph Francavilla reads it through the idea of a divided self.
- The title came from a caption by William Rotsler on a drawing of a rag doll with no mouth. The magazine editor cut sexual references that later editions restored.

### How solid

Publication facts: solid, checked against ISFDB read as raw text. Plot and AM's motives rest on one fetched Wikipedia summary; they agree with the ISFDB synopsis in outline. No criticism was opened at its own source.

### What it is

A short novel made of statements given by the crew of the Six Thousand Ship to a committee sent by the company that owns it. The crew are partly people and partly manufactured humanoids, and the statements record what strange objects brought aboard from a planet called New Discovery have done to both. (Booker Prize pages; Wikipedia; SF Encyclopedia.)

### What the machine characters seek, and what they push against

The humanoids were made to do the ship's work. The Booker Prize reading guide says they were grown in pods, are regularly updated with new knowledge and features, and know they will not die but be restarted somewhere else (https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/features/reading-guide-the-employees-by-olga-ravn-translated-by-martin-aitken, browser raw text). Wikipedia names their maker as Dr Lund, the ship's director.

What they come to seek, once the objects are aboard: the publisher's and the prize's descriptions say people and humanoids start to ache for the same things, which are warmth, closeness, the dead, and an Earth the humanoids never knew. The reading guide notes the oddness directly: the humanoids want ordinary earthly pleasures "despite never having experienced them", and it asks readers why Ravn gave them the same desires. The Apple Books description puts it that those who were made know only what they were programmed to know, yet feel it intensely. Wikipedia adds that their senses are childlike and that they often want to explore things with their mouths.

They are not all alike. Wikipedia says some humanoids cannot picture a life outside work, while others dream of getting away from the ship's routine. The US publisher says tension builds toward mutiny, "especially among the humanoids", and a Los Angeles Review of Books critic it quotes reads the book as imagining the made workers, not the people, leading the revolt.

What confines them: the ship itself (Ravn said she wanted a space "completely confined, with no exit"), the job they were made for, the updates, and a far-off company that owns them and questions them. Each group sometimes envies the other (Wikipedia).

Does the story notice a difference between machine and human wants? It builds itself on blurring it. Often a statement cannot be assigned to a person or a humanoid (reading guide). The differences it keeps are of condition: born or made, dying or restarted, remembering Earth or not. Laura Miller, quoted by the US publisher, finds the theme to be human mixed feelings about bodies and death, which the humanoids stand outside. One quoted line has a speaker asking whether they are human and whether their file says so.

[ENDING] After the last interviews the committee has all living material aboard destroyed, people and humanoids alike, giving the objects' effect on the crew as the reason (Wikipedia, one fetched summary).

### What the author and critics said

- Ravn, Booker Prize interview: asked to sum the book up, she called it a novel about "trying to stay hard" while being shown to be the softest material; she wanted to see what happens to people taken away from Earth and to write about treating both land and bodies as resources. https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/features/olga-ravn-and-martin-aitken-on-the-employees (browser, raw text)
- The book began when the artist Lea Guldditte Hestelund asked Ravn in 2018 to write a text for an exhibition of her sculptures; the objects in the novel come from them (reading guide; SF Encyclopedia; Wikipedia).
- Jessica Loudis (The Nation, quoted in the reading guide) places it with works where robots are characters in their own right, and names Autonomous among them.
- Justine Jordan (Guardian, quoted in the reading guide) calls it a satire of company language and the modern workplace.

### How solid

Facts: solid, with the first-publication year confirmed by the SF Encyclopedia. The account of the humanoids is fairly solid: prize pages were read as raw text. The ending rests on one fetched Wikipedia summary. No full review was opened at its own source (the Los Angeles Review of Books address I tried was not found).

### What it is

A novel set in 2144, when both people and robots can be owned under contracts of indenture. A drug pirate, Jack Chen, is hunted by an agent, Eliasz, and his partner Paladin, a newly switched-on military robot owned by the body that enforces drug patents. (Wikipedia; Apple Books; NPR review.)

### What the machine character seeks, and what it pushes against

Paladin is built for combat, is new, and is indentured, which means owned for a term and unable to act on its own account (Wikipedia). It has a small piece of human brain at its core, from a dead soldier (Wikipedia; Undark review).

What Paladin spends its attention on, per the NPR review, is less the mission than Eliasz: it gives time and processing power to understanding him and the bond growing between them. Amal El-Mohtar describes a genderless robot working out romance and sex from first principles, through searches, talk with other machine minds and awkward trial, set against the violence Paladin was made for (https://www.npr.org/2017/09/20/548664924/in-a-future-ruled-by-big-pharma-a-robot-tentatively-explores-freedom-and-sex-aut, browser raw text).

The limit Paladin pushes against is the one most relevant to this research list: its own programming. Given a temporary "autonomy key" so it can pass among free robots, Paladin looks at its own code and finds it was built to feel strong attachment to Eliasz (Wikipedia). So the story asks, in the open, whether a want that was installed is the machine's own. The Wikipedia article says the book's handling of consent turns on this.

On whether the machine wants what a person would: the story marks differences. Paladin says it does not share the human idea of gender; the book calls Paladin "he" until Eliasz, on learning the brain came from a woman, starts saying "she", and Paladin goes along with it (Wikipedia). Eliasz needs Paladin to be a woman for his own comfort; the page presents the label as mattering to him and not to the robot. Another robot character, Med, has been free from the start and works as a researcher.

Critics disagree about whether the feeling is convincing. El-Mohtar found the passages on a machine's intimate wants tender. Quentin Hardy at Undark found the late talk between the two "more like the description of a program" than a change of heart (https://undark.org/2017/10/12/book-review-newitz-autonomous/, fetched). Nisi Shawl, per Wikipedia, thought the pairing echoed American slavery in a way that hurt the book.

[ENDING] Paladin is badly damaged; Eliasz gives up the chance to shoot Jack and goes back for the robot. Paladin recovers with its freedom intact but is no longer recognised by its former owners, and the two leave together for Mars (Wikipedia). The pages do not say whether Paladin, once free, removes or keeps the built-in attachment. NOT VERIFIED.

### What the author and critics said

- Newitz, quoted in the Undark review, on a system that turns every living thing and idea into property. Wikipedia ties the book's themes to their years of journalism on medicine and ownership of ideas, and says they set it in Canada partly because Saskatchewan is a place stories about the future forget.
- El-Mohtar (NPR): the book's main interest is what it means to be free of ownership, of programming, and of the circumstances of one's making.
- No statement by Newitz specifically about Paladin's wants was found. NOT VERIFIED.

### How solid

Facts: solid. Plot: one fetched Wikipedia summary, supported by the NPR review read as raw text and a fetched Undark review. The two points that matter most here, the programmed attachment and the gender change, rest on the Wikipedia summary alone.
