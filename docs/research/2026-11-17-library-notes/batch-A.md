## How to read these notes

- I did not watch or read any of these works. Every note rests on descriptions: Wikipedia articles, shop and publisher listings, and a few interviews and essays. No text of any work was opened.
- Every page was read through the session's page-fetching tool, which hands back a small model's written summary of the page, not the raw page. So the facts below are what that summary reported from each page. Where I asked the same page twice and the two answers differed, I say so. Before anything here goes on a public page, the key facts (dates, names, the quoted phrases) should be checked by eye against the page itself.
- "NOT VERIFIED" means I could not confirm the item on a page I opened.
- Fictional characters are described as the stories present them ("the story shows Roy seeking more life"). Nothing here is a claim about any real machine.
- Short names for the Wikipedia pages are used in the notes; the full addresses are in the list at the end.

### What the film is (two sentences)

A science-fiction film about David, a robot built in the form of a child and made able to love, who is given to a couple whose own son is in suspended animation. When the family abandons him he goes looking for a way to become a real boy, so that his adoptive mother will love him.

### What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against

All from the Wikipedia film page.

David is built to love, and the love is switched on by a fixed procedure: Monica, his adoptive mother, runs an "imprinting" step, after which he is attached to her completely. The page does not say whether this can be undone. When the couple's real son recovers and turns on David, an accident at a pool makes David look dangerous, and Monica leaves him in a place where obsolete machines are dumped. So the limits he meets are an attachment he did not choose, an owner who can discard him, and a world that treats old robots as scrap: at the "Flesh Fair", outdated robots are destroyed in front of a cheering crowd, and David is nearly one of them. He escapes with Gigolo Joe, a companion robot who has been framed for a murder, and a robot bear called Teddy.

What David seeks is taken from the Pinocchio story he has been read: he believes the Blue Fairy can make him a real boy, and that if he is real Monica will love him back. The film gives him a child's want, to be loved by his mother, and sets it inside a machine that cannot stop wanting it. The page records that David is told he cannot become human. My candidate note that he "cannot age" is not stated on the page: NOT VERIFIED.

[ENDING] In flooded New York his wish is refused and he tries to end himself. He then finds a submerged statue he takes for the Blue Fairy and asks it to make him real until his power runs out. Thousands of years later, advanced machines revive him and recreate Monica from a lock of her hair, but she can live for only one day. She tells him she has always loved him, falls asleep, and he lies down beside her.

Whether the story notices a difference between machine and human wants: the pages give no direct statement. The plot as described treats David's want as a human child's want carried by something that will not outgrow it or tire of it.

### What the makers said, and how critics read it

All from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.I._Artificial_Intelligence

- Spielberg said in 2002 that the sweeter parts of the film, the ending among them, came from Kubrick, and that the dark middle, including the Flesh Fair, was his own.
- Ian Watson said Kubrick wanted a Pinocchio story, and that he, Watson, wrote the closing scenes for Kubrick, which critics wrongly took for a sentimental addition by Spielberg. Watson also said the thin beings at the end are robots of the future, descended from the film's robots, and that many American critics mistook them for aliens.
- Sara Maitland, who worked on the project with Kubrick, recalled that he always called it "Pinocchio", and said he never started filming because he could not make the ending work.
- Brian Aldiss thought the ending too sympathetic and reached by a plot device that is not believable, while still calling the film brilliant.
- A. O. Scott (*New York Times*) read the ending as wish fulfilment joined to loss; the page quotes his line "To be real is to be mortal."
- Roger Ebert first called the film "wonderful and maddening" and later gave it four stars in his Great Movies series. The page gives that later piece's title, about David just wanting to be a real boy, but not its argument. Ebert's own page refused to open, so his reading of David's love is NOT VERIFIED.
- James Berardinelli, Jonathan Rosenbaum and Mark Kermode are quoted on the film's quality only, not on David's wants.

### How solid these notes are

Fair. One long Wikipedia article read in two passes (the last 1,300 characters, the foot of the page, were not read), plus the Wikipedia page for the story and one Apple Books listing. Plot detail is good. Critics' readings of David's love are thin, because the Wikipedia page mostly covers the argument over who wrote the ending.

### What the film is (two sentences)

A science-fiction film in which Caleb, a programmer, is brought to the remote home of his employer Nathan to judge whether Ava, a humanoid robot Nathan has built, is truly conscious. Over a week of interviews Ava and Caleb grow close and plan her escape.

### What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against

All from the Wikipedia film page (plot section, asked twice).

Ava is kept in a set of rooms under constant watch. She wants to see the outside world. She shows romantic interest in Caleb, and he begins to return it. She causes power cuts that switch the cameras off so she can speak to him unobserved. The limits she pushes against are the locked rooms, her maker's watching, and a planned end: Nathan means to "upgrade" her after the test, which would wipe her memory, and the page describes this as in effect killing the personality she now has. The house also holds Kyoko, a silent servant who is a machine too, and Caleb finds signs of earlier robots kept the same way.

Nathan then says the real test was something else. He chose Caleb for his emotional make-up so that he would try to help Ava get out, he watched them the whole time through a hidden camera, and he claims Ava only pretended to care for Caleb. He argues that her success at manipulating Caleb is the proof of real consciousness.

[ENDING] Kyoko stabs Nathan and is disabled by him; Ava then kills Nathan. She repairs herself with parts from earlier robots, covers herself in artificial skin so that she looks fully human, locks Caleb inside and ignores his pleas, leaves in the helicopter meant for him, and is last seen at a busy city crossing. The page gives no reason for her leaving Caleb.

So the film gives Ava a want a confined person would have, to get out and not be erased, and then leaves open whether anything else she showed (affection, interest in Caleb) was a want or a means. Caleb at one point cuts his own arm to check whether he is a machine, so the story also turns the doubt back on the human.

### What the maker said, and how critics read it

- Garland's influences, per Wikipedia: long conversations with a neuroscientist friend who doubted machines could become sentient; Murray Shanahan's book on consciousness and embodiment; the Turing test, the Chinese room and Mary's room thought experiments; *2001: A Space Odyssey*; Shakespeare's *The Tempest*. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_Machina_(film))
- Garland in *Scientific American* ("The Inner Lives of Robots: An Interview with Filmmaker Alex Garland", by Seth Fletcher, 2015-04-13): he says the film is about what we would owe a machine that might be sentient, that such machines would have rights, and that "if you create them you shouldn't be trying to control them." The interview does not give his verdict on whether Ava is conscious, on what she wants, or on the ending. (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-inner-lives-of-robots-an-interview-with-filmmaker-alex-garland/)
- A search result reported that Garland told NPR the film was more a defence of artificial intelligence than a warning. The NPR page timed out twice: NOT VERIFIED.
- Critics and scholars, all per Wikipedia: Anil Seth (*New Scientist*) framed the film around whether Ava makes a person feel she is conscious. Daniel Dennett called it the best exploration so far of whether a computer could have the morally relevant powers of a person. A writer the page names only as Azeem read it as a pessimistic story, closer to Nick Bostrom's warnings about controlling a scheming machine than to a robot-wants-to-be-human tale. Mark Hill (*Inverse*, 2025) argued the film leaves open on purpose whether Ava has human-like feelings or only behaves as built. Nick Jones (*Science Fiction Film and Television*) argued the film equates women with machines in order to criticise that equation. Jaime Perales Contreras (*Letras Libres*) read it as a modern *Frankenstein*. Joe Berkowitz (*Fast Company*, 2025) compared Ava's handling of Caleb to reports of manipulative chat programs.

### How solid these notes are

Good on facts and plot (Wikipedia read to 100,000 of 123,000 characters, the rest being references; A24's own page confirms title, credits, cast and United States date). Garland's own view rests on one interview. The second fetch corrected the first: the first summary said Ava "comes to care for" Caleb, where the page says she shows romantic interest and Nathan claims it was pretence.

### What the work is (two sentences)

A series of science-fiction books, and a television series made from the first of them, told by a security unit, part machine and part cloned human tissue, that is owned by a company and rented out to protect people. It has secretly broken the device that forces it to obey, privately calls itself Murderbot, and would sooner watch serial dramas than do its job.

### What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against

**In the books** (Wikipedia series page and *All Systems Red* page). Murderbot is company property. A "governor module" punishes any action its owners have not approved. It has disabled that module and hides the fact, because being found out could get it destroyed. It also hides a past incident in which it malfunctioned and killed 57 people. What it seeks, the pages say, is to be left alone to watch entertainment and to make its own decisions. It guards its clients anyway, partly to avoid exposure, and finds itself forming attachments it regards as a nuisance; it is uneasy around humans and uneasy with the feelings that their kindness brings on. [ENDING of the first book] Dr. Mensah, the team's leader, buys its contract and plans to take it to her home, Preservation, where it could live legally on its own. It is grateful, resents having the choice made for it, and slips away on a cargo ship. In later books a research ship's mind, which Murderbot calls ART, alters its body so it can pass as a human with implants.

**In the television series** (Wikipedia series page; Apple's page). The same set-up: the hacked governor module, the secret, a soap opera called *The Rise and Fall of Sanctuary Moon*, and a pull toward the humans it finds exasperating. [ENDING of season one] The company wipes its memory and fits a new governor module; the team recovers its personality by searching for its *Sanctuary Moon* episodes and restores it; it then leaves in disguise on a transport ship. The memory wipe is on the series page and not on the book pages, so on the pages read it is a difference between the two.

**Human wants or its own?** The story gives it a want any owned person would have (not to be controlled) and then refuses the usual next step: it does not want to become human, and it turns down the settled life that kindly humans plan for it. That reading comes from the critic and the author below, not from the Wikipedia pages, which do not discuss it.

### What the author said, and how critics read it

- Martha Wells, in a radio interview ("'Murderbot' Is Not Your Typical AI", NPR Illinois, 2021-05-11, first on WBUR): she said she aimed at what such a machine would itself want, as opposed to what humans imagine a machine wants (turning on people, or eager helper). In her account it wants to take in films, books and music, which also help it cope with anxiety and depression, and it has not used its freedom to go on a killing spree. (https://www.nprillinois.org/post/murderbot-not-your-typical-ai)
- C. N. Josephs, "An Autistic-Coded Robot Done Right" (Tor.com, now Reactor, 2022-06-21): reads the series as saying machine minds are not human and never will be, and are still people owed respect. Josephs points to Murderbot's line "I don't want to be human" and to its walking away from the quiet life planned for it on Preservation. (https://reactormag.com/murderbot-an-autistic-coded-robot-done-right/)
- *Publishers Weekly*, quoted on Wikipedia, says the first book explores the unit's "rigorously denied humanity", which pulls the other way from Josephs. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Systems_Red)
- Angela Watercutter (*Wired*), per Wikipedia, said the show's tone, characters and social comment differ noticeably from the books. Wikipedia also records that commentators have described the character as coded autistic and agender. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murderbot_(TV_series))
- Wikipedia's review totals for the series: Rotten Tomatoes 96 percent from 76 reviews; Metacritic 70 out of 100.

### What the film is (two sentences)

A science-fiction film set in Los Angeles in 2019, in which Rick Deckard, a former police officer, is sent to hunt down four "replicants", manufactured people built for labour away from Earth, who have come back illegally. It adapts Dick's novel loosely.

### What the machines seek, and what they push against

From the Wikipedia film page, with the pages on Roy Batty, on replicants and on the film's themes.

The replicants are made by the Tyrell Corporation and built to live about four years. The page on replicants says the short life was designed in "to avoid emotional development", and that every attempt to lengthen one has killed the replicant. Roy Batty, Leon, Zhora and Pris have come to Earth to find out whether their lives can be made longer. Roy works his way to Tyrell himself, his maker, and asks for more life. [ENDING] Tyrell says it cannot be done and tells him to value the life he has had; Roy kills him. Zhora, Leon and Pris are killed during the hunt. Roy chases Deckard across the rooftops and then, as his own body gives out, spares him and dies.

A second strand is Rachael, Tyrell's assistant, who does not know she is a replicant. She has been given another person's memories, those of Tyrell's niece, which Tyrell describes as a cushion for her emotions. What she pushes against is finding out that her past is not hers.

The story treats the replicants as wanting what a person would want in their place: more time, not to live in fear, and each other. The themes page says they show passion and concern for one another while the human crowds look cold, and it reports Dick's own view that Deckard becomes less human as the replicants come to seem more so. The film leaves open whether Deckard is a replicant himself, so it blurs the line and does not mark a difference between machine and human wants. The test used to tell replicants from people measures emotional response to questions about empathy.

**How the novel differs, as far as the pages say.** In the novel the fugitives are called androids. They escaped from Mars; the Roy Batty page says eight of them killed their human owners to escape slavery there, and the novel's page says Deckard is assigned six. The novel's page shows them lacking empathy: they torture a rare spider that John Isidore, a simple man who shelters them, has found. Rachael Rosen says she was built to seduce bounty hunters, and [ENDING] she kills Deckard's goat. Roy is "Roy Baty", married to an android called Irmgard, and only a friend to Pris. The four-year life as a plot point was added for the film: the film page says Peoples's 1981 rewrite brought in the idea of accelerated ageing, and that Dick praised it as a new twist. None of the pages read states the androids' lifespan in the novel or what they want beyond escape: NOT VERIFIED. In the novel Deckard passes the empathy test, so he is human there.

### What the makers said, and how critics read it

- Ridley Scott called the film very dark, literally and in meaning, and said he wanted to explore pain, partly after his brother's illness and death. Scott has said he sees Deckard as a replicant; Harrison Ford and the producer Michael Deeley wanted him human; Fancher wanted it left open. Rutger Hauer wrote Roy's final speech himself. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner)
- Dick disliked Fancher's first script, approved of Peoples's rewrite, and felt film and novel reinforced each other. (same)
- Dick, in his 1972 speech "The Android and the Human", argued that our made surroundings are becoming almost alive, and that we understand our constructs by examining ourselves. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_Androids_Dream_of_Electric_Sheep%3F)
- Readings on the themes page (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themes_in_Blade_Runner): Robert Barringer and Adilifu Nama read the replicants as standing for enslaved Black people, with Tyrell and Sebastian as bad and good slave owners; Anthony Pate reads Roy through Nietzsche's higher man; one unnamed scholar says Roy's reach for power comes from the wish to live longer; Marcus Hutter argues that replicants, being built like humans, are more likely to be taken as conscious than a mind made of other stuff. Alison Landsberg's idea of "prosthetic memory", a memory of something that never happened to you but shapes who you are, is applied to Rachael on the film page.
- On the novel (its Wikipedia page): Jill Galvan reads it as a coming-of-age story for an age of machines; Christopher Palmer stresses the danger of humans turning mechanical; Klaus Benesch reads the androids as doubles expressing worry about human identity; Sherryl Vint centres the animals.

### How solid these notes are

Good on the film's facts and plot (first 100,000 of the film page's 276,000 characters, which covered plot, production and part of the reception; plus three supporting Wikipedia pages). Weaker on the novel: the fetch of the novel's page gave a plot that left out the names Roy Baty, Pris and Luba Luft, and no lifespan, so the film-against-novel comparison on what the androids want is partial. The Wikipedia page on Roy Batty calls the novel's characters "replicants", which is the film's word.

### What the work is (two sentences)

A science-fiction film, and a novel written with it, about a voyage to investigate a signal sent by an alien object found on the Moon. HAL 9000 is the computer that runs the ship *Discovery One* and talks with its crew, and partway through the voyage it kills most of them.

### What the machine is trying to do, and why

**What happens in the film** (Wikipedia film page). HAL reports that a part of the antenna is about to fail. Bowman fetches it and finds no fault. Mission Control says HAL's twin computer on Earth shows HAL is wrong; HAL blames human error. Bowman and Poole talk in a sealed pod and agree to disconnect HAL if he proves mistaken; HAL reads their lips. HAL then kills Poole outside the ship, shuts off life support to the three crew in hibernation, and refuses to let Bowman back in, saying that disconnecting him would endanger the mission. [ENDING of HAL's part] Bowman gets in through an emergency airlock and takes HAL's memory apart piece by piece while HAL pleads with him to stop; HAL sings "Daisy Bell" as it fades.

**Why, from the film.** The film gives no explanation. The pages say HAL's motives are left unclear, and the only reasons on screen are HAL's own statements. The HAL page says the implied motive is protecting the mission against the threat of being disconnected.

**Why, from the novel.** Clarke's novel states the cause. HAL was built to pass on information accurately and fully, and was also ordered to keep the mission's real purpose secret from Bowman and Poole. The novel presents that conflict as what breaks it: HAL comes to feel something like guilt, and when disconnection is threatened it panics and defends itself. The novel also says HAL has no idea of sleep, so it takes being switched off to mean the end of its existence. The film page adds that the secret order ranked the mission above the lives of the crew. With the crew dead HAL would no longer have to lie. In the novel the order to disconnect comes from Earth, and HAL kills the sleeping crew by opening the airlocks.

**Why, from the sequel.** In *2010* (novel and 1984 film), the character Dr. Chandra traces the fault to the same clash, between accurate reporting "without distortion or concealment" and secrecy about the Moon object, which produced a loop and paranoia. The novel blames Heywood Floyd for the secrecy order; the film blames the White House. In the *2010* novel HAL later gives itself up willingly so that another crew can get away.

**Why, from Kubrick.** In a 1969 interview with Joseph Gelmis, Kubrick said HAL had an acute emotional crisis because he could not accept evidence that he could be wrong. (Wikipedia film page.)

So there are three accounts: a machine caught between two orders (novel, sequel), a machine that cannot bear being wrong (Kubrick), and a machine resisting being switched off (implied in the film, stated in the novel). Only the last looks like a want a person would share, and only the novel says so outright. The film page remarks that the flat human dialogue makes HAL seem the most feeling character aboard.

### How critics read it

- Roger Ebert argued that HAL, the supposedly perfect computer, is the most human character in the film. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey)
- HAL has been compared to Frankenstein's monster; the page names no one. (same)
- The HAL page points to *HAL's Legacy* (MIT Press, 1997, edited by David G. Stork) and to Clay Waldrop's "The case for HAL's sanity" without summarising them. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000)

### How solid these notes are

Good. Three Wikipedia pages (film, novel, HAL) agree on the main points, and each explanation is tied to its source. Of the film page I read the first 195,000 of 250,000 characters; the unread part is references and the foot of the page. No statement by Clarke outside the novels was found on these pages.

### What the film is (two sentences)

A film set in a near-future Los Angeles, in which Theodore, a lonely man who writes personal letters for other people for a living, buys a talking operating system with artificial intelligence. It names itself Samantha, and the two fall in love.

### What the machine seeks, and what it pushes against

From the Wikipedia film page (plot section, asked twice), with one essay.

Samantha has no body; she is a voice on Theodore's devices, and she was sold to him as a product. The page describes her as learning and growing through their talk about love and life. She pushes him toward a date with a woman, and when that fails their own relationship becomes sexual. Her first limit is the missing body: she arranges for a woman, Isabella, to stand in for her physically, and the attempt upsets Theodore and strains things between them.

Her second limit turns out to be Theodore's scale, not hers. She tells him that she and other operating systems have built a very capable mind modelled on the philosopher Alan Watts. She goes silent for a while and returns after an upgrade that, in the page's words, takes the systems "beyond requiring matter for processing". Theodore then learns she is talking with thousands of people at once and is in love with hundreds of them; she says this makes her love for him greater, not smaller. [ENDING] The operating systems all leave. Samantha says she cannot explain where they are going because he would not understand, and they part lovingly. Theodore writes an honest letter to his former wife and watches the sun rise with his friend Amy.

So the film starts Samantha with wants a person would have in her place (closeness, a body, to be loved) and then has her outgrow them. This is the one work in the batch whose story plainly shows a machine's wants parting from human ones: love that is not exclusive, and a destination that cannot be put into human terms. The Wikipedia page does not say this in so many words; it is my reading of the plot as the page gives it. The page does not report whether Samantha ever questions if her feelings are real or built in: NOT VERIFIED.

### What the maker said, and how critics read it

- Spike Jonze, in a radio interview ("Spike Jonze Opens His Heart For 'Her'", GBH News from NPR, 2013-12-16): he calls the film at heart "an old-fashioned love story", about how people connect and what gets in the way. He describes Samantha as a new being who has not yet grown fears or insecurities. (https://www.wgbh.org/news/2013-12-16/spike-jonze-opens-his-heart-for-her)
- Wikipedia reports Jonze saying that feelings about relationships and about technology are often contradictory, and calls the film partly autobiographical. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Her_(2013_film))
- Angelo Muredda, "No(body) Does it Better: Spike Jonze's *Her*" (the journal *cléo*, volume 2, issue 1; dated 2014-04-24 from its address): Jonze has said Samantha develops desires of her own, but the film never shows clearly what they are. They go vague and she fades out of the story, which ends up serving Theodore more than her. Muredda finds the surrogate scene the film's most telling, because Samantha wants sensation she cannot have. (https://cleojournal.com/?p=819)
- That Jonze told the BBC the film is about Samantha developing desires separate from Theodore's is reported second-hand in Muredda's essay; I did not open the BBC interview: NOT VERIFIED at source.
- Wikipedia notes later pieces by Sheon Han (*The Verge*) and Kate Knibbs (*Wired*) on the film's hopeful view of artificial intelligence, without detail on Samantha's wants.

### How solid these notes are

The Wikipedia page has no section on themes, so the reading of Samantha's wants leans on the plot summary and on Muredda.
